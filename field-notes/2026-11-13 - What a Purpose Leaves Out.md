*Friday, November 13, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 28 minutes 13 seconds recorded*

## Prompt

> What does a purpose leave outside consideration, even while I am trying to care?

This morning's prepared questions ask me to look beyond the tree I have made so familiar. They also ask me to revise yesterday's account of return. The unexpected part begins with a doubt about a branch’s origin, which I can now correct through comparison with the oak.

## Field Notes

It's clear and cold. I report about 45 degrees, though it feels chillier. Some yellow foliage remains, and bright fallen leaves lie over older brown litter. The climbing dome stands in its new position. The exposed plant circle remains, with small fruits on the growth identified as liriope.

*Photograph available on the [illustrated field-notes site](https://ecologicalintentionality.org/sits/2026-11-13).*

*November 13. Open branches against blue sky, remaining yellow foliage, low sunlight and the dome at right. The photograph records an arrangement, without establishing what the move has done for the tree.*

Looking toward a neighboring oak, I notice a break high in its canopy. From this more open view, its position makes me question the origin of the fungus-bearing piece by the compost pile that I had associated with the walnut. I walk over to that piece and look again. I now suspect it may have come from the oak.

**Clarification added after the review, November 13:** This is the piece I removed on October 28 and placed near the compost pile. I can confirm that it came from the oak: the break on the detached piece matches the broken limb still in the oak canopy, along with its texture and color. The second photograph in my field record shows that oak break. I couldn't see it at the time of the removal because the walnut's foliage blocked the view. Through the walnut's now-bare limbs, the break is perceptually available. I didn't witness the fall; the comparison establishes the piece's origin without making that past event something I saw happen.

At the plant circle, I see foliage that could provide shelter. I don't see anything wintering there during this inspection. A brief look cannot establish that nothing is present.

## Reflection

### A branch from the oak

The walnut has become familiar enough to organize my attention. The branch I removed from it on October 28 had grown from the neighboring oak. It was lodged in the walnut; that location hadn't established its tree of origin. The October 4/11 fungus-bearing piece is a separate episode. I also remember noticing a different texture when I handled the October 28 piece and explaining it through decay. The newly visible break lets me correct that account.

The completed removal stays in the record, while my description of what I removed changes. The October 28 note's inability to locate the source of the break also stays as a record of what was available then. The walnut's loss of foliage has changed what I can see, and the comparison of the breaks, texture and color now supports a definite correction. Whether removal benefited the walnut remains another question.

### Seen, and not considered

When I look back over the week, the sentence I'd build the case around came at the tail end of Wednesday's sit, and nobody scripted it. I'd seen the plants under the dome for two years. I mowed around them on purpose. And I planned for three days to move the dome without once asking what would happen to them. They were in plain view the whole time, and they weren't part of what I was deciding.

So a thing can be right there to see and still have no place in what I'm doing. My care was aimed at the walnut's root, and the aim told me what mattered. Other lives can help shape what I intend. What I intend then decides which lives I never think about. It works in both directions, and the second happened while I was trying to do right by a tree.

This also sharpens the week's practical question. Other lives can help form what I intend, while the purpose I take up can exclude other lives from deliberation. The groundcover was visible while I planned the dome move, yet its future was outside the concern organizing that plan. Ecological formation can be selective. Being formed through a place and remaining answerable to that place are different achievements.

### What I asked, and what I heard

Yesterday I said I'd ask the children three things: why they wanted the ring left in the first place, what they want to happen to it now, and whether they want a say. I asked before supper. One of them shrugged and handed the question back to me. The others wanted the plants left, with mowing to be talked over in the spring. All of them wanted a say. Their reasons had to do with play, with the stories they tell about the ring, and with how the plants look. Mine had to do with care for the plants and the tree. When I described what I'd read about cutting liriope back, the answer shifted: if it's good for the plants, maybe cut them once over the winter.

I'm reporting this from memory and in my words, so it can't stand as a record of theirs. People can keep one practice going for different reasons, and doing the same thing together doesn't show a shared purpose. Hearing them also made their part in deciding more explicit in what I intend. They had purposes for the ring before I asked, and had already participated in decisions about the yard. What I can report now is a change in how I receive their reasons; a jointly revised purpose or a change in who has authority to decide would need its own evidence.

### Two repairs to the return

Yesterday I overclaimed twice, and both need mending in public.

First, I wrote that nothing in the aim or the act could have brought the ring into my purposes, and that only coming back could. That's too strong. If I'd felt the dome dragging across those plants halfway through the move, I could've stopped right then, and the act would've corrected itself. If someone had asked "what about the ring?" the night before, the plants would've entered the plan before I lifted anything. Neither happened. So here's the version I can stand behind. On Wednesday, the return is what did the work. More generally, coming back gives the lives and reasons a purpose left out another chance to shape what happens next. It's one of the ways that happens, and I don't yet know whether it's the main one.

Second, I gave a purpose three positions: who it's for, who it touches, and who it leaves out. I wrote as though they were separate boxes, and they overlap. The ring was touched by two years of shade and mowing-around and left out of my planning, both at once. I also put play in the wrong place. Keeping the dome good for play was part of what I meant to do from the start. So the three work better as questions I can ask of anything I'm about to do. Whose good is this for? Whose conditions does it change? Whose life, or whose reasons, never came up?

A return that changes nothing can still be a good one. What matters is whether evidence against my plan had a real chance to count.

### Formed by a place, and answerable to it

I've been running two things together. One is how a relationship with other lives shapes what I come to intend. The other is whether what I intend stays open to being corrected by them. The first can go badly. A purpose can be shaped by a place and still be selective, or stubborn, or self-serving. Months beside this walnut formed the care that moved the dome, and that same care left the ring out.

So ecological intentionality can't be my name for the mornings I get it right. It has to cover the three days I didn't consider the ring as well. Formation is about where a purpose comes from and how it gets its particular shape. Answerability is about whether it can still be changed by the lives it affects and the people who share it. An account that described only the second would be describing good behavior and calling it a theory of consciousness.

### Turning around

To test this on something other than the dome, I asked the three questions of my purpose this morning. I'm out here to get material for a paper. That's mostly for my good. It changes conditions for the yard in small ways: where I stand, what I leave alone, what I move. And what never comes up? I could list the soil's microbes or the air, but a list like that is easy, and it costs me nothing.

So I turned around and looked, as the prompt had asked me to do. It had named the oaks among the things I might inspect. The oaks and the red maple stand a few yards away, and I walk past them every morning. Next to the walnut they felt less familiar, which surprised me. In the recording I also say that I consider them. My familiarity has developed unevenly across this shared place. That raises a related question to the ring: what does sustained attention make familiar, and what continues in the background? It doesn't yet show that I left the oaks out of a particular practical decision, or that caring for the walnut caused their neglect.

That's when I saw the break in the oak. Reasoning about what my purposes hide hadn't supplied this particular doubt. It took a thinner canopy, a prompt to look elsewhere, and a walk across the yard to the piece itself. Which is one more reason the children's answers matter. Somebody else's reasons, or a change in what's visible, can show me what mine left out.

### A conflict I don't have yet

Earlier this week I had to withdraw the phrase "a real case of goods in conflict." This morning I thought I might be near one. If liriope benefits from a late-winter cut, and if standing foliage shelters small creatures through the cold, then cutting for the plant's sake and leaving it for whatever lives there would pull against each other.

At the tree I said I'd probably cut this one patch and leave the leaf litter everywhere else. I also said I had two things to find out first: whether the cut is for the plant's good or for how it looks to us, and whether anything is wintering in there. The reading I did afterward, described below, weakens the first premise. So I still don't have a conflict. I have a question to settle before February, and a plan I shouldn't act on until it's answered.

## Research Connection

[Gregory Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) makes the strongest challenge here. In his two essays on conscious purpose in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, purposive selection extracts partial sequences from wider circuits. He also discusses discrepant organismic purposes and possible correctives through love, art, contact with nonhuman nature and religion. I can't claim either selective attention or its possible correction as a discovery beyond him. The wording I half-remembered at the tree, that consciousness sees only the short arcs of circuits that purpose directs it to, comes from a third essay in the same book, “Style, Grace, and Information in Primitive Art.”

Bateson even supplies my example. In “Conscious Purpose versus Nature” he asks what happens to the picture of an oak wood when it's drawn only to answer questions of purpose. In “Effects of Conscious Purpose on Human Adaptation,” his fourteenth point describes purposive reasoning as a straight line laid over a world of loops, and his nineteenth lists what might correct it. Contact with animals and the natural world is on that list, hedged with “perhaps” and “sometimes.” The essay ends with the voice from the whirlwind asking Job about wild goats and calving deer. I take that as his way of saying that narrow purpose is an old problem.

A second predecessor comes from phenomenology. Alfred Schutz, as Michael Barber presents him in *The Routledge Handbook of Phenomenology of Agency*, holds that once I settle on what I'm acting in order to bring about, that aim spreads relevance through everything connected with it. Some things become topics and the rest stay on the horizon. Schutz also describes relevances that an agent hasn't chosen and that can interrupt a project or shape its conditions from the outset. They can lead someone to revise, limit or abandon earlier purposes. In those terms, the ring became topically relevant after the move. Calling it an imposed relevance would be a further interpretation to test, since I was considering it through an existing concern for the yard. The oak's break likewise brought an overlooked feature into my account of the branch. So “seen but not considered” already has a careful description in Schutz as well as a diagnosis in Bateson.

That leaves me a narrower question, and I think a better one. Revision of a purpose already belongs to Schutz's account. I need to ask how another creature's possible good comes to count as a reason for that revision, and how the reason remains answerable to the creature rather than my preferred story about it. Bateson says contact with the natural world sometimes breeds wisdom. I'd like to trace when it does, through what changes, and what could show that my account is mistaken.

The constructive task for [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) is more specific: explain how an encounter changes the beneficiary, means, projected fulfillment or governance of a particular intention. If this turns out to articulate Bateson's account in phenomenological terms, that would be a result to acknowledge. [Attention](../concepts/Attention.md) and [remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md) help keep the present view, its inferred history and its independent consequences distinct.

Post-sit horticultural reading also changes the practical question. University of Georgia's *Liriope Culture in Georgia*, Bulletin 755, treats annual foliage removal as optional and discusses condition-dependent reasons for cutting. The possibility of a late-winter cut therefore needs an actual rationale in this patch. It can't yet stand as a demonstrated conflict between plant welfare and winter habitat. [Archived university bulletin](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/25263/files/B755.pdf).

At the tree I connected the cutback to [Robin Wall Kimmerer](../concepts/Robin%20Wall%20Kimmerer.md), and the connection is worth making carefully. In “Mishkos Kenomagwen: The Teachings of Grass” in *Braiding Sweetgrass*, Kimmerer describes research requested by Indigenous basket makers, whose harvesting knowledge gave her and her graduate student Laurie the question to investigate. Harvested sweetgrass plots thrived over two years while the untouched control plots declined. “Sweetgrass thrives where it is used and disappears elsewhere.” Some plants do better with a human hand in their lives, and leaving alone isn't always the caring choice. But the chapter also says that plants differ, that some are easily harmed by harvest, and that the task is to know each one well enough to respect the difference. I can't carry sweetgrass's answer over to this patch. Liriope isn't a true grass, and I said at the tree that Clemson's guidance shows pruning helps, which was more than I knew. What Kimmerer gives me is the form of the question. Is this a plant that needs me, and how would I find that out from the plant?

For the Ecology of the Cross, I am developing a separate proposal: participation in Christ's reconciliation could form communities willing to revisit their purposes and undertake material repair. Colossians connects the church and creation with resurrection and the cross; this proposed communal practice is an extension to argue. [Colossians 1:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1%3A15-20&version=NRSVUE).

The gardener in Luke asks for time joined to cultivation. Yet the parable retains possible cutting in verse 9 and stands in a context of repentance. It challenges impatience without clearly abandoning fruitfulness. A theology of patient care has to face that tension, rather than making the tree's uncertain future validate my proposal. [Luke 13:1–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+13%3A1-9&version=NRSVUE).

The Greek supports part of what I said at the tree and limits the rest. The owner says ἰδοὺ τρία ἔτη ἀφ' οὗ ἔρχομαι ζητῶν καρπόν (13:7): for three years I keep coming, seeking fruit. The verb is ἔρχομαι, and the participle ζητῶν is from ζητέω. He's a man who returns, and each return asks the same thing. The gardener answers, Κύριε, ἄφες αὐτὴν καὶ τοῦτο τὸ ἔτος, ἕως ὅτου σκάψω περὶ αὐτὴν καὶ βάλω κόπρια (13:8): leave it this year also, until I dig around it and put on manure. ἄφες is the aorist imperative of ἀφίημι, the ordinary verb for letting go, leaving and forgiving. It's the word in the Lord's Prayer (ἄφες ἡμῖν, Luke 11:4). The related noun ἄφεσις names release in Luke 4:18 and in the Septuagint's jubilee legislation, Leviticus 25:10. That word family opens a possible theological connection with [Wednesday's sit](2026-11-11%20-%20Done%20Isn%27t%20Finished.md). The seventh-year land sabbath in Leviticus 25:1–7 and the fiftieth-year jubilee in 25:8–12 belong to the same chapter but are distinct provisions. A shared word family doesn't by itself establish an intended allusion in this parable. Norman Wirzba's *Agrarian Spirit* reads forgiveness in the Lord's Prayer through that same release of debts and land.

In 13:8 the verb means “leave it.” I said at the tree that it's the same word as “forgive,” and it is, but I can't show that Luke intends the echo here. The owner reports repeated visits seeking fruit; the gardener asks for another year in which to dig and add manure. He proposes cultivation, but the parable doesn't narrate that work or its result. He also retains the possibility of cutting (ἐκκόψεις αὐτήν, 13:9). The relation to my own return is an interpretation to develop: patience can change how a purpose is pursued without yet abandoning its end. The year remains open, as the effects of the dome move remain open to me.

## Question Carried Forward

What evidence could make me revise the purpose itself, and how will I recognize when I have only become more effective at pursuing it?
