*October 3, 2026 · Black walnut · 19 minutes*

## Prompt

> How is the past present in this living form without being directly visible or personally remembered, and what further work turns a visible trace into an accountable history?

## Field Notes

I stood close to the trunk beneath a gray, overcast sky. Deeply grooved bark filled the foreground. The trunk divided into great limbs of different widths and angles; forks and knots interrupted their lines; mostly green leaves gathered beyond them.

What I saw was present structure: thickness, grooves, forks, knots, unequal limbs, and canopy. Knowing this as a living tree, I also know that the trunk and branches developed through growth over time. That developmental claim is not the same thing as visually perceiving the earlier growth. The bark supplies no caption for its determinate history. A knot might follow storm, pruning, obstruction, disease, ordinary branching, or several conditions together. Its visibility makes a question possible without establishing the answer.

## Reflection

The encounter sharpened a restraint within [Attention](../concepts/Attention.md): **visible trace is not self-explanatory history**. Looking carefully can distinguish a groove, knot, wound, fork, or irregular branch. Knowing the walnut as a living tree lets those present features raise questions about development through time, but perception does not replay that development. It cannot by itself tell whether a particular feature came from storm, drought, pruning, competition, disease, or repair.

Different claims require different evidence. Biological expertise might explain how the tree grew around a feature. Earlier photographs or memory might date it. Testimony might identify an act of pruning. Weather records might connect it to a storm. The cause might remain unknown. The walnut’s present living form can bear consequences of a history without narrating that history.

This adds another form of [Remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md). **The past is present enough to matter and absent enough to require inquiry.** Some of that absence may yield to records, expertise, photographs, or testimony. Some histories are carried through communities and practices beyond one person’s recollection. Some causes may never be recovered. These are different limits and should not be gathered into one vague mystery.

The same restraint matters at the scale of [Place and Agency](../concepts/Place%20and%20Agency.md). A photograph cannot explain why land is divided as it is, why a road runs where it does, or why environmental harm is distributed unevenly. Those relations can be real without being directly visible, but claims about them require archives, maps, law, testimony, environmental data, and political analysis. Attention can hand a question to another method; it cannot replace that method.

## Research Connection

[Maurice Merleau-Ponty](../concepts/Maurice%20Merleau-Ponty.md) offers one account of how a past remains active in the present. In *Phenomenology of Perception*, acquired worlds remain available through sedimentation but must still be taken up in present activity; human action can also become deposited in a cultural world, and a lived past can acquire weight as the atmosphere of the present. These are claims about human bodily, cultural, and historical existence. They do not license the claim that the walnut’s morphology is itself a Merleau-Pontian sedimentation. The tree poses a different problem: its present living form bears consequences of development that perception cannot fully reconstruct.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) gives the tree’s particular becoming metaphysical weight. In *Finite and Eternal Being*, the actual course of a plant’s development depends partly on the natural situation in which it grows. Later, Stein says that external influences can release, impede, or counteract formative powers and treats the “fate” that befalls an individual as significant for its formation. This is what this walnut has become here. Yet the present form does not tell the whole causal story, and what formed a being is not thereby good for that being.

[Melanie Harris](../concepts/Melanie%20Harris.md) changes the question asked of history. Ecowomanism foregrounds Black women’s lived experience, eco-memory, intergenerational sharing, environmental justice, agency, and praxis. Its pressure cannot be reduced to a reminder that history matters. For this project, Harris raises the harder questions of whose experience and memory guide ecological knowing, which histories and harms require investigation, and what ecological knowledge is meant to do.

Together these differences produce a methodological sequence: **past operative in the present is not yet established historical explanation, and established historical explanation is not yet accountable response**. Merleau-Pontian sedimentation, Steinian living development, and Harris’s ecowomanist concern with memory, justice, and praxis are not three versions of the same process. The sequence is a constructive proposal emerging from the fieldwork, not a formula borrowed from any one thinker.

The sequence also presses [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md). If it names only increasingly adequate perception, it is too small. Even historically informed perception may be too small. It may need to name a disciplined ecological orientation that coordinates perception, memory, biological knowledge, historical evidence, community knowledge, ethical judgment, and praxis; or the project may eventually need another term.

## Question Carried Forward

**What evidence, memory, relationship, and action does this history actually require, and when would knowing the history change responsibility?**
