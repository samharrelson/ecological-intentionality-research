*September 30, 2026 · Black walnut · 24 minutes*

## Prompt

> Can received being educate reception without pretending to dictate it?

## Field Notes

A large branch lay beneath the walnut. Fungi were visible along part of the fallen wood, with walnuts and yellow leaves scattered nearby. The canopy remained mostly green in clear morning light. The whole tree shared the yard with neighboring trees, fence, house, truck, and a barn under renovation.

The branch’s present condition did not disclose its full history. It may have been damaged, diseased, or dead before it fell, but the photograph alone cannot decide among those possibilities. What appeared was simpler: branch on ground, fungi on wood, living tree behind it, and a changed scene within a familiar place.

## Reflection

The branch was available. That did not mean I had been invited to take it. Its presence left open questions about decay, habitat, squirrels, fungi, use, and the history of the place. **Availability is not permission.**

That ordinary hesitation became a test for a larger thought: if I understand both myself and the walnut as finite beings whose existence is received rather than self-grounded, does that make non-possession follow? It does not. Dependent beings can dominate. Creatures can exploit other creatures. **Metaphysical dependence is not ethical virtue, and received being is not yet receptive knowing.**

The stronger proposal therefore has to become weaker. To understand oneself as received may *form* a creature toward receiving another without possession. It may make possession less philosophically innocent and weaken the assumption that because I can take something, I may take it. But it does not decide what care requires. That judgment still needs biological knowledge, history, practical context, and attention to the particular being involved.

This changes Attention. The theological idea does not reveal a hidden property called *creatureliness* in the walnut. At most, it may alter how I inhabit the encounter. The test is whether it returns me to this tree, with its fallen branch, fungi, history, proper good, and relations, or lets a beautiful category replace it. Remainder includes that resistance: the walnut exceeds my view, knowledge, use, and theological description.

## Research Connection

Edith Stein supplies two distinct disciplines against possession. In *On the Problem of Empathy*, another’s experience is given as another’s while remaining non-primordial for me: I do not possess it from within. In *Finite and Eternal Being*, around p. 61, finite existence is received rather than possessed as self-originated. One claim concerns the givenness of another; the other concerns the metaphysical condition of finite being. Their convergence around non-possession is a constructive proposal here, not a conclusion Stein draws for vegetal ethics.

Later in *Finite and Eternal Being*, Stein gives a more explicitly theological route through love and self-giving. Around pp. 350–351 she describes love as self-giving rather than possession, and around pp. 457–458 she connects surrender to God with a surrender of the self to the created world. That may supply part of the missing bridge, but it changes the argument. The bridge would run through participation in divine love, not through bare finitude alone, and Stein’s full account of love remains personal. The walnut still does not give me a ready-made vegetal ethic.

Husserl keeps the proposal taxonomically honest. If something changes in this encounter, is it perception, attention, valuing, motivation, practical intentionality, horizon, affect, or habit? A theological discipline may reorganize how existing intentional structures are inhabited without becoming a newly discovered phenomenological act. This gives Ecological Intentionality a demanding possibility: it may be less a special act than a practice of coordinating distinct modes of knowing and responding without allowing one to impersonate another.

Robin Wall Kimmerer keeps creaturely language answerable to particular beings, places, histories, and practices. Walnut is not sweetgrass, cedar, alder, squirrel, soil, child, or river. Gift, reciprocity, and care cannot be inferred from a shared category of creation. They must earn their names in concrete relations.

Vanessa Watts presses from another direction. Even a less possessive human perceiver can remain the architectural center of the account: I receive, I attend, I am ethically formed. Her account of Place-Thought within specifically Haudenosaunee and Anishnaabe traditions challenges the priority granted to that starting point. The walnut cannot remain merely the occasion for my spiritual development.

The resulting proposal belongs to Vegetal Encounter as a test rather than a synthesis: inhabit a metaphysics of received being as a discipline of attention, then allow the particular tree, its relations, and the limits of one’s knowledge to correct the discipline.

## Question Carried Forward

**How should a being that possesses neither itself nor another absolutely learn to live among other finite beings? What additional premise or practice could make that question ethically determinate?**
