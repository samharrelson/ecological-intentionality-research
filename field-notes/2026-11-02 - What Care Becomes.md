*November 2, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 42 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> How does care become specific enough to guide an action, and what could change my understanding of it afterward?

## Field Notes

The upper branches are increasingly exposed. Yellow foliage remains lower in the canopy, and fallen pieces lie among the green plants around the trunk. Looking from the roots toward the branches, I notice different qualities in the bark. The light may contribute to that difference.

A leaf within reach looks yellow and spotted. I expect it to fall, remembering others that have fallen. That expectation belongs to the encounter, but it is not the same as watching this leaf detach.

*November 2. Exposed upper branching and remaining yellow and green foliage against a gray sky; neighboring canopies overlap the view.*

At the places where the swing's straps used to hang, smoother indentations remain visible. I associate them with the straps because of their location and what I remember. Their appearance does not tell me whether the tree was harmed or whether removing the swing helped.

A saw sounds in the background. Birds call, and a squirrel draws my attention toward acorns. Near the end, the sky brightens. Looking up from the same place, I can see more through the canopy. I cannot attribute that changed view entirely to leaf loss; the illumination has changed too.

## Reflection

Care begins to acquire content when it becomes a question about this life and this situation. Wanting to care for a tree doesn't yet tell me whether to intervene, leave something alone, wait, or seek better knowledge.

The earlier swing sequence makes that practical. Concern for the walnut made removal worth considering. A spider's web on a strap gave me reason to delay. Later, when the web was no longer apparent, I removed the swing. Returning to the branch opens another inquiry into what that action meant for the tree. These are decisions across encounters, made with incomplete knowledge of several lives.

Today I also question an ordinary practice: leaving fallen foliage near the walnut. I have understood that as care. What knowledge would support or revise that understanding? A familiar practice needs to remain as answerable as a new intervention.

Even the biological inquiry may concern **more than one life**. Black walnut litter contributes to soil processes, but its chemistry may matter differently to plants sharing the ground. [Iowa State Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/can-i-use-woodchips-walnut-mulch-my-landscape) and [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/allelopathy-in-the-home-garden) offer differently cautious guidance about walnut-derived mulch and juglone-sensitive neighbors. I haven't identified all the plants here or tested the consequences of my practice, so I can't infer that leaving or clearing the litter is the right response. The point is to let relevant knowledge help determine what care requires.

## Research Connection

The positive proposal I am developing through [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) concerns how practical purposes become determinate through relations with independently living beings. Its sequence is: an adopted purpose, apprehension of other lives, specification of what to pursue, action or deliberate waiting, and continued correction. Another being need not share my intention for its condition to matter to that sequence.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) helps develop and challenge this account. In *On the Problem of Empathy*, III.5(i), her gardener apprehends the condition of plants, investigates causes, and seeks ways to influence them. [Hal King](../concepts/Hal%20King.md) develops this practical dimension in [“Propagating Plant Empathy with Edith Stein”](https://doi.org/10.3197/whppp.63876246815941), drawing on gardening, propagation, water, soil, and sunlight. His interpretation is a constructive resource as well as a serious challenge to any claim that plant empathy stops at recognition. The comparison therefore begins with an account that already reaches into action. Moving from recognition to care cannot by itself establish my proposal's originality.

The further question is how concerns for several lives specify a purpose and its timing. Perhaps one continuing commitment to care explains the changes through new beliefs about what to do. Perhaps the end itself becomes more specific or is replaced. I need to distinguish those possibilities. This account of a purpose developing over time also leaves open the stronger question of what realizes a particular conscious act.

The parallel *Ecology of the Cross* proposes that participation in Christ's reconciling life can transform communal purposes. Its [provisional theological statement](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/5) gives distinct scriptural starting points for that claim. A congregation's grounds offer a concrete question: what evidence about affected lives could change the purpose of a project? That requires ecological inquiry, historical knowledge, and accountable deliberation. The branch does not establish the theology, and self-giving must never become a demand that vulnerable others bear the costs of an unchanged institutional aim.

## Question Carried Forward

**What would show that another life changed the end I pursue, rather than its means or timing—and what evidence could make me revise what I call care?**
