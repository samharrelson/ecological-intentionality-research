*October 5, 2026 · Black walnut · 20 minutes*

## Prompt

> How can one world be genuinely shared when it is never received in exactly the same way? What is common here without becoming the same?

## Field Notes

Monday morning was overcast and cool, around 63–64°F. The forecast rain had mostly failed to arrive; after a little weekend drizzle, little moisture remained visible beneath the walnut.

Several leaflets occupied nearly the same small region. Most were green, while some had begun to yellow. They met the diffuse light at different angles. Some faced me, some turned away, and some were partly hidden behind others. Small holes, marks, surface differences, light, shadow, and orientation were directly visible. Their causes were not.

## Reflection

The close view sharpened a discipline within Attention: **difference is given before its explanation is**. One leaflet’s yellowing or another’s holes might involve development, light, insects, wind, disease, or some combination, but the photograph does not decide among those possibilities.

Stepping back changed the scale. The differing leaflets were perceptually gathered as a canopy and a tree. That does not yet mean perception alone establishes organismic membership. Husserl can explain much of the unity of one perceived thing; biological knowledge and Stein’s later metaphysics do further work when these leaflets are understood as differentiated members of one living organism. The walnut, oak, fungi, soil, animals, perceiver, fence, and power lines do not belong together in that same way. Place and Agency must distinguish perceptual unity, organismic membership, ecological relation, and ethical community rather than allowing “one field” to name them all.

The same broader atmospheric situation also enters bodies differently, and even the local physical conditions may differ from one leaflet to another. Light changes what I see; temperature reaches my body through human capacities; each leaf occupies a different position and orientation. I did not measure local light, moisture, airflow, or temperature, so those differences remain questions rather than findings. **Shared atmospheric situation does not imply identical local exposure. A shared world does not imply one experience or one subject.**

This changes Remainder. Difference may not be only what intimacy fails to overcome. It may belong positively to relation itself. Intimacy could occur partly through noncoincidence rather than by progressively eliminating it: **difference need not mean separation; belonging need not mean coincidence**.

## Research Connection

Husserl already provides rich accounts of perceptual synthesis, horizon, embodiment, and the lived body. *Ideas II* §36 makes the comparison especially demanding: when one hand touches the other, Husserl describes doubled touch-sensations and the Body as both physical thing and sensing Body. Merleau-Ponty therefore does not discover reversibility where Husserl had only separation. The question is what changes when a doubled structure within lived-body constitution becomes an ontology of flesh. The leaflets becoming “the walnut” likewise remain a Husserlian problem before any stronger ecological or ontological claim is made.

Edith Stein explains how differentiated members can belong to one internally articulated living whole. Her account of non-primordiality preserves a different distinction: when another subject’s experience is genuinely at issue, that experience does not become primordial to me. The walnut sit does not by itself establish vegetal experience in that sense. Organismic membership and foreign experiential givenness are therefore different questions, and the plant case keeps the second one open.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty makes the comparison less tidy. In *The Visible and the Invisible*, flesh does not culminate in fusion. Distance and thickness belong to access; reversibility remains incomplete; and distinct bodies do not become organs of one huge animal. His working notes also describe the sensible as diacritical and speak of an “advent of difference,” but one note says that this occurs on a ground of resemblance. Difference may therefore belong internally to relation without being established as an absolute first term. The remaining question is whether this ontological divergence can also distinguish the goods and vulnerabilities of particular living beings.

Melanie Harris adds a methodological interruption. Different leaf positions cannot serve as a metaphor that establishes environmental injustice, and even local physical exposure would need measurement if I wanted to establish it scientifically. Human environmental injustice requires another sequence: **shared condition → evidence of patterned unequal exposure or vulnerability → historical and structural explanation → warranted injustice claim → accountable response**. Harris helps orient that inquiry toward lived experience, environmental justice, method, and praxis; none of those transitions is automatic.

For Ecological Intentionality, the result is a method of disciplined transitions. Perceptual belonging is not organismic membership; organismic membership is not ecological belonging; ecological belonging is not ethical harmony; and differentiated exposure is not yet injustice. The source work also adds another restraint: difference may be internal to relation without that yet proving a metaphysical priority of difference.

## Question Carried Forward

**What kind of difference does belonging preserve, and what further evidence turns differentiated exposure into a warranted claim about vulnerability, injustice, or responsibility?**
