## Prompt

> What can returning to a changed place reopen about what I intend, and about whose reasons matter?

Yesterday I moved the climbing dome and committed to leaving the exposed plant circle through winter. Today I come back to a place that has continued overnight without waiting for me to explain it. My prepared questions concern return, care and shared purpose. The morning gives those questions some particulars.

## Field Notes

It's sunny. I report a temperature in the mid-50s, warmer than the script had anticipated. Most of the upper canopy is bare, though foliage remains at the left from my usual position. I remember leaves on particular branches yesterday that I no longer see there. I didn't watch them fall.

## Reflection

### Care can keep its purpose while reconsidering its means

Last night I read about liriope maintenance. [Clemson's guidance](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/liriope/) includes cutting foliage before new growth in late winter. That makes an alternative thinkable. It does not establish that cutting this mixed patch would be the best response.

I haven't decided to cut it. The winter commitment still stands. What has changed is my sense of what I need to learn before treating restraint as a complete account of care. The question includes the liriope, its neighboring plants, possible inhabitants and the walnut. An ornamental maintenance practice and care for that whole situation need not have the same objective.

This gives the distinction between means and ends a practical setting. I can remain concerned for these plants while reconsidering what would serve them. But I should not announce a changed action simply because another action has entered consideration. Today I have a possibility to investigate.

### Three moments: aim, act, return

Here's the piece I've been trying to build for the paper. An ecological intention has three moments: the aim, the act and the return.

The **aim** comes first, and it does more than point at a goal. It organizes what counts as relevant. When I planned to move the dome, my care was aimed at the walnut's root, and for three days that aim decided what I looked at and what I looked past. The plants in the ring were in plain view the whole time. They weren't part of what the aim was asking about.

The **act** changes the situation the aim was formed in. Dragging the dome ten feet fulfilled one stretch of the intention and, in the same motion, uncovered ground the intention hadn't considered. An act is never only the carrying out of a plan. It sets something down in a world that's already underway, and what I set down takes part in things I didn't arrange.

The **return** is the third moment, and it's the one I'd been treating as scheduling. I come out here every morning, and I'd thought of that as the practice's calendar. This morning I think it belongs to the structure of the intention. If an intention only aimed and acted, [yesterday's](2026-11-11%20-%20Done%20Isn%27t%20Finished.md) story would have ended when I let go of the dome. What kept it open was that I turned around and looked at the ground. The return is where what I did meets what's there, and where something I never considered can become something I have to answer for.

### Why the return matters

Three things make me think the return deserves its own place and isn't a repeat of the aim.

First, the situation I come back to is no longer the one I planned in. The aim was formed before the act. The return meets a world that includes the act and everything that has gone on since without me. The ring had a night without the dome. The canopy dropped leaves I didn't watch fall. I come back to a yard I've already changed, and this is the first morning I can say that.

Second, the return is where an intention becomes answerable. I can aim at another creature's good and act on it without ever finding out whether I was right. Yesterday I leaned on Husserl's distinction between the will at work in an action and a resolve that reaches past it. The return is how that longer resolve stays in contact with evidence. Without it, "care" would describe my state of mind and say nothing about the walnut or the liriope.

Third, the return doesn't have to be a walk across the yard. Last night's reading was a return. Asking someone else what they think will be a return. What makes it one is that I bring a purpose back into the presence of something that can correct it.

I need to keep one overstatement out of this. At the tree I said the return is where reception happens. That's too exclusive as it stands. I receive the world before I aim and while I act, or I couldn't aim at anything in particular. The claim I can defend is narrower: the return is where reception can reach the purpose itself and reopen what governs the next act.

### Three positions around a purpose

A purpose also has a shape around it. There's who it's for. There's who it touches without meaning to. And there's who it leaves out.

When I planned the move, the walnut was who it was for. Play in the yard was something the move touched, and I'd thought about that. The ring of plants was left out altogether, though I could see it. Yesterday the ring moved from the third position to the first: plants that hadn't figured in the plan became the stated reason for a winter of restraint.

Nothing in the aim or the act could have produced that movement. The aim had already settled who mattered, and the act followed the aim. It took coming back. So here's the strongest version of today's claim, stated so it can be tested: **the return is how anything moves between those three positions.** A life gets from "left out" to "touched" to "for" when someone comes back and finds it there.

The dome's new site gives me a small check on that. It now stands on fallen leaves over ground where I've mowed grass for years. I've already said I'll mow around it in the spring and let what grows under it stay. So the pattern from the old ring is starting again, this time with my eyes open. Whatever I set down takes part in something, and I'd like to know what before another two years go by.

### What would count against this

A claim like this has to say what would defeat it. If the return only ever confirms what I came out expecting, then it's a habit of reassurance and the third moment does no work. If I can't point to mornings where coming back changed what I meant to do, the structure is decoration.

So far I have one clear case, and one case isn't a structure. I also have to allow for the opposite error. A return that changes nothing can be exactly right if the evidence supports staying the course. What would count against the account is a return that shuts out unwelcome evidence, and I won't always be the best judge of when I'm doing that.

### Reopening, without a required revision

The important part is the reopening. I may return through looking, reading, listening or receiving evidence that arrives later. The return need not end in a new conclusion. It can give me better reasons to continue. Nor does reception wait until the third moment: the world affects what I can intend before and during action as well.

Yesterday's ring helps me ask what happens when an action is finished but its consequences keep entering the question of what to do next. Today's plant research adds another form of encounter. Knowledge can mediate a relation without making the living being disappear behind the source. The actual plants remain capable of showing that my understanding was incomplete.

This is the positive account of [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) I want to develop. Return is a way a purpose can remain answerable to independent lives through time. It isn't yet a demonstrated new form of consciousness. Ordinary learning, inquiry and motivated agency may explain much or all of this sequence. The account has to specify what the living relation contributes, rather than claim novelty from the fact that I came outside again.

### A practice can be shared before its reasons are understood

Who asked for the ring to be left, and why? That question reaches beyond identifying plants. The history of a shared place includes requests, routines and decisions whose reasons may not belong to the person doing the work.

Someone can carry out a practice for years and later adopt a different justification for it. The outward action may stay the same. Yet that person's new reason does not automatically become everyone's reason. A shared purpose cannot be established simply by giving an existing practice a more compelling interpretation.

The next step is to ask. What did the practice mean to the people involved? What do they want now that the arrangement has changed? Are they interested in deciding together? Those are questions for an actual conversation. I haven't had that conversation yet, so I have no answers to report.

The plants' condition and other people's reasons enter the inquiry differently. Observation and ecological knowledge help with the former; listening is indispensable to the latter. Agreement among people would not prove biological benefit. A thriving patch would not prove agreement. Both can matter to what a shared intention becomes.

That is also a form of [intimacy with remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md). Familiarity with a place or a practice does not make its lives or its participants' reasons transparent. Returning can make the relation more particular while leaving room for an answer I have not supplied.

## Research Connection

Two earlier sits already used the word return, and today changes how I read them. [Can Restraint Be a Return?](2026-09-22%20-%20Can%20Restraint%20Be%20a%20Return.md) asked about return as giving back, in Robin Wall Kimmerer's sense of reciprocity, where a gift calls for something offered in response. [What Returning Makes Present](2026-10-18%20-%20What%20Returning%20Makes%20Present.md) asked what repeated visits let me perceive. Today's return is a third sense: coming back to a purpose after acting on it. I don't want to merge the three. They may belong together, though, if giving back to a place begins with coming back to see what my purposes did there.

The strongest existing account of something like this third moment is Merleau-Ponty's notion of institution, which I'm rereading alongside the late work on flesh. As the introduction to *Time, Memory, Institution: Merleau-Ponty's New Ontology of Self* (Morris and Maclaren, 2015) presents it, an instituting event opens a field in which later experiences make sense as its sequel, and what's instituted keeps being taken up again and reworked. That already describes an act whose meaning stays open to what follows. So the sequence of aim, act and return isn't new as a temporal structure, and Husserl's motivated inquiry and Bateson's corrective circuits of learning explain a good deal of it too. If ecological intentionality adds anything here, it'll be in what the return answers to: another creature's actual condition and other people's actual reasons, neither of which my purpose authored.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) makes the communal question more demanding. The [Stanford Encyclopedia discussion of her social philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stein/) describes communal experience as constituted through individual contributions, without a separate group ego. This remains a secondary source check; I have not located the primary “Individual and Community” passage for a fresh close reading. A repeated practice gives me a question about a genuine *we*, not its answer.

Three secondary readings in my library fill in what that means for a case like this one. Tullius (*On the Ethical Philosophy of Edith Stein*, 2024) and Sobrak-Seaton (*Spirit, Expression and Community in the Philosophy of Edith Stein*, 2024) both work from Stein's example of a military unit grieving its leader. Each member's grief is their own, and it's also lived as ours. The community is carried by its members and never becomes a subject standing apart from them. Sobrak-Seaton also cites Antonio Calcagno's point that Stein describes this as solidarity and doesn't posit a separate we-intentionality, which I'll need to respect when I write about a communal *we*. Alasdair MacIntyre's chapter on "Individual and Community" adds two things I can use. Members can contribute to one communal end from different motives. And Stein grades communities by how far their members take responsibility for the common mind, down to cases where present members inherited it from the past or share in a common life without yet bearing responsibility for it. That's close to what I'm asking about a practice begun on request and carried on by habit. It also keeps me from deciding in advance that different reasons mean no community.

The theological inquiry turns to [John 1:14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A14&version=NRSVUE) and its language of flesh and dwelling among us. [Niels Henrik Gregersen](../concepts/Niels%20Henrik%20Gregersen.md) gives that question a more developed argument in the introduction to [*Incarnation: On the Scope and Depth of Christology*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt13wwwk5.4). Selected primary passages checked after the sit connect Jesus' particular embodied life with a wider creaturely scope, while distinguishing divine presence with creatures from treating every event as an incarnation of God.

The Greek gives the question its edges. John 1:14 reads καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν. The noun is σάρξ (flesh; σὰρξ as it stands in the verse), where the prologue could have used ἄνθρωπος (human being), a word it uses elsewhere. The verb is ἐσκήνωσεν, the aorist of σκηνόω, to pitch a tent or dwell, from σκηνή (tent). And the dwelling is ἐν ἡμῖν, "among us" or "in us." At the tree I kept coming back to that small word *among*. Elizabeth Johnson makes the observation about σάρξ in her essay in Gregersen's volume, "Jesus and the Cosmos: Soundings in Deep Christology," and she adds a limit right away: the flesh the Word became was specifically human. I'd add a second limit from the verse itself. The "us" of ἐν ἡμῖν are the ones who go on to say "we have seen his glory" (ἐθεασάμεθα τὴν δόξαν αὐτοῦ), a community of human witnesses. So the verse can open the question of how far σάρξ reaches. It can't tell me whether that reach includes a ring of groundcover I didn't plant and might not have chosen. I don't know, and I'd like the reading to tell me.

For the Ecology of the Cross, I propose that participation in Christ could form communities willing to revisit the consequences of their purposes, especially for lives they overlooked. That is a further constructive argument. The word “flesh” alone does not establish it, and the ring cannot prove it. An imposed loss does not become redemptive because the person acting intended care.

I am also carrying a question to unfinished Maximus reading: can transformed participation preserve creaturely difference? The proposed distinction between a nature and its mode of existence needs close textual work. It cannot be reduced to the contrast between a species name and a garden arrangement, or used to suggest that changing a plant's conditions leaves it unaffected.

Maximus's terms are λόγος φύσεως (the principle of a nature) and τρόπος ὑπάρξεως (its mode of existing). Polycarp Sherwood's *The Earlier Ambigua of St. Maximus the Confessor* points to Ambiguum 42 (PG 91:1341D) for the rule: innovation concerns the mode, while a nature's λόγος stays what it is, since a λόγος altered would be that nature's corruption. I've located the passage through Sherwood and haven't yet read it in Constas's Greek and English. It already corrects something I said at the tree. I said that nothing I'd done touched what the plants are. Maximus's distinction is about how a nature can come to exist in a new way without ceasing to be itself. It gives me no license to say that a mower, a dome or two years of shade leave an organism unharmed. I also said that I think the cross preserves the remainder and transforms it. That's the proposal I'm carrying to the text, and the text may refuse it.

For a congregation, the practical beginning could be a return to the reasons behind an existing use of its grounds. Who initiated it, who maintains it, what lives with its effects, and what would be needed to reconsider it together? This is a proposed inquiry with social, ecological and theological obligations. It isn't a reported congregational result.

## Question Carried Forward

When I return, can the plants' condition and other participants' reasons change what governs my next act, including giving me better reasons to keep the same purpose?
