*October 30, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 44 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What carries forward from these encounters when I leave the tree?

## Field Notes

Before recording, I looked toward the walnut and saw yellow leaves falling one after another, more quickly than I had expected. I had been occupied with other things. The movement caught me anyway.

Returning beneath the canopy, I find blue sky and sunlight where yesterday there was muted cloud cover. Green leaves remain among the yellow. Open spaces reveal sky and smaller branches. Leaves continue to fall.

*October 30. Sunlight through a mixed green and yellow canopy; the swing remains attached below. The still image records appearance, while the recording documents movement.*

A squirrel appears close by while I am looking upward. For a moment we face each other, then it scurries away. I greet it and imagine what it might make of this human beneath the tree. The imagined answer is mine.

Acorns fall from the surrounding oaks. Their sounds interrupt the reflection, as does the crunch of acorns and leaves underfoot. Later one strikes my shoulder. That interruption is immediate and quite uncomfortable. The walnut is the focus of the sit, but it is not the only thing here capable of changing what I attend to.

## Reflection

Sunlight and falling leaves appear together this morning. That does not tell me that sunlight caused this particular burst. In the recording I also struggled to distinguish what I was calling individual leaves from the walnut's compound leaves. Botanical descriptions of black walnut explain that its leaflets can fall separately, so I may have been following individual leaflets rather than whole leaves. [Biological knowledge](../project/sources-and-reading.md) helps correct that description without telling me what triggered each detachment. Nor does recognizing the same walnut through a thinning canopy tell me everything about how its living organization persists.

I keep returning to the distinction between a change in the tree, a movement I perceive, and the significance that movement acquires for me. Calling all three relational leaves their differences unexplained.

There is also the longer change I have been trying to describe: returning here seems to affect how I approach other situations. A familiar habit of asking what I cannot know about another life sometimes becomes relevant beyond the yard. I now have particular responses to examine, rather than only a general feeling of greater attentiveness. But a remembered response and its explanation are different kinds of evidence.

That matters for [attention](../concepts/Attention.md). A falling acorn can interrupt me in an instant. A practiced willingness to reconsider another being's situation develops over time. I should not assume they work in the same way.

## Research Connection

[Gregory Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) makes the comparison more demanding. In “Form, Substance, and Difference,” he places mental process in relevant circuits of differences, including pathways beyond the skin, and explicitly includes memory (*Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, Jason Aronson reprint, pp. 465–467). A later encounter carrying effects of earlier activity is therefore not something his account simply overlooks.

My constructive proposal for [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) is that relationships with independently unfolding lives may help form what we can notice, intend, and become willing to revise. The stronger claim concerns their role in realizing a particular conscious act. It needs a specific account of that role, not only testimony that a meaningful practice has changed me.

I've also added two sources to [the reading record](../project/sources-and-reading.md) that complicate this work. Simon P. James's [“On the Plurality of Nonhuman Worlds”](https://doi.org/10.1093/monist/onag029) distinguishes biological diversity from cohesive experiential worlds, and it does not decide whether plants are conscious. Andrew Trevor Kirkpatrick's *Whitehead and Merleau-Ponty: Toward a Process Phenomenology* (2020 doctoral thesis, with a separate 2026 book) offers a processual interpretation of flesh and challenges any easy division between Merleau-Ponty's early and later work. I still need to assess his arguments beyond the abstract and contents.

Learning, prior commitments, embodied habits, and philosophical reading remain serious explanations. The walnut's life is not exhausted by its meaning for me, but recognizing that independence does not settle which explanation succeeds.

## Question Carried Forward

**How does a sustained relation with another living being carry into a changed response elsewhere—and what would distinguish its contribution from learned habit, motivation, and memory?**
