*November 1, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 29 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What becomes visible after an action, and how can another living being change what I intend?

## Field Notes

The sky is gray, and yellow stands out in the thinning canopy. I took photographs earlier in the morning and returned to record. There are fallen leaves beneath the walnut after yesterday's yard work. I did not witness the overnight falling, but a few leaflets fall while I stand here. More sky appears between the branches than I remember from recent mornings.

Yesterday I removed the swing. Today, looking at the places where I remember its straps hanging, I notice smoother, indented areas of bark. The attachment points are available to look at in a different way now that the straps are gone.

An earlier hesitation also comes back to me. When I considered removing the swing in October, a spider had made a web on one of the straps. I delayed the work because I did not want to disturb it. Later I noticed the web was absent. I do not know what became of the spider, but that changed my judgment about when to act.

## Reflection

The swing had a purpose: play. The branch has a continuing life within the walnut that exceeds that use. Returning to the tree has made that difference increasingly consequential to me.

Concern did not supply one instruction in advance. At one point it gave me a reason to wait for the spider. Later, concern for the walnut supported removal. These were judgments about different lives, made with incomplete knowledge. The missing web did not certify that no spider would be affected, just as removing the swing did not certify that the tree benefited.

The bark marks add something to the encounter after the action. I associate them with the straps because of where they appear and what I remember. I still need to distinguish a visible indentation from an injury to living tissue. The marks cannot retroactively become the evidence on which I made yesterday's decision.

## Research Connection

The positive account I am developing through [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) concerns how a purpose takes shape through encounters with beings whose lives are independent of it. An initial use becomes open to reconsideration; another life makes a different action matter; a commitment becomes specific enough to guide action or waiting; subsequent encounters can reopen it.

This sequence makes the proposal concrete. Wanting to care does not yet specify whether to remove something, leave it, or wait. A relationship can help determine those particulars without either participant sharing the other's intention.

**Here is the positive structure I want to explain:** a family purpose, recognition of other living interests, a delay in response to the spider, a later decision for the walnut, and a renewed need to learn from what the action reveals. Care was not an instruction I possessed in advance. It acquired particular commitments across encounters with different lives, even while my wider wish not to cause harm may have remained the same. I want to distinguish changing the purpose itself from specifying its content and choosing its timing.

[Raymond Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) gives the branch–swing distinction philosophical weight. In *Neofinalism*, chapter 5, he describes human organizing activity extending into tools and machines (pp. 36–37), while distinguishing an organ's activity from a tool constructed and inspected from outside (p. 44). His account helps me ask what belongs to a living being's own organization and what belongs to the purposes imposed through its use. It does not tell me whether this attachment harmed this walnut or whether removing it was the best response.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) gives me another question about living individuality and what remains unavailable from another life. Her distinct account of non-primordial access doesn't tell me what the walnut or spider experiences, or whether either benefited from my decision. But it makes me wary of identifying their lives with the uses I make of them.

The strongest challenge to my proposal is ordinary practical reasoning: perhaps I retained one general end, avoiding harm, and changed my beliefs about means and timing. That would still be a meaningful response, but it would narrow the claim about revised ends. I need to show which part of the purpose changes. Explaining how concern develops over weeks also differs from showing what realizes a particular conscious act in the moment.

All Saints' Day gives the parallel theological work a particular urgency. In the *Ecology of the Cross*, I propose that participation in Christ can transform what a community pursues, allowing affected lives to question its established plans. The [provisional theological statement](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/5) develops distinct biblical starting points for that argument. A congregation considering its land use would need to ask whose lives bear the costs and whose evidence could change the plan. The swing supplies no proof of that Christology, and generous intentions do not settle the consequences of communal action.

## Question Carried Forward

**When another life changes whether and when I act, has it changed my purpose, made that purpose more specific, or changed how I pursue it?**
