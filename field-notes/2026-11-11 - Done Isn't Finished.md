*Wednesday, November 11, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 19 minutes 49 seconds recorded*

## Prompt

> After I carry out an act of care, what can the changed place ask me to reconsider?

I came out with a prepared question about the difference between completing an action and fulfilling its purpose. Today there was a completed action to consider.

This is the fourth morning in a row that the climbing dome has been part of the sit. On November 8 I noticed it standing near an exposed root and wondered whether it belonged there. On November 9 I named an intention to move it for the walnut's sake and tried the structure to see whether I could shift it alone. On November 10 it was still where it had been, and I spent the morning asking how an intention can be definite while its future stays open. This morning I'm standing on the far side of the action, and that changes what I can ask. I'm looking at what I did, and at what it left.

## Field Notes

The morning is sunny and chilly. More leaves lie beneath the walnut than remain on it. Most of the yellow still attached is on the lower limbs; higher branches open onto blue sky.

I'm out later than usual again, and the light is already strong. Yesterday I was surprised by how quickly the lower canopy had opened. Today the surprise has settled into something closer to recognition: this is what the tree looks like now, and the yellow that's left won't be here long. A storm is forecast for the weekend, with cold behind it. That's a forecast, and I'm holding it as one.

Before recording, I moved the climbing dome about ten feet from its previous position. I had been considering that move for several days out of concern for the walnut, while wanting to preserve the structure's use for play. The move is complete. Whether it helps the tree remains unknown.

Where the dome stood, a ring of green plants surrounds a relatively bare center. I look at the leaves and soil and photograph the patch. Some foliage looks like what I have been calling lilyturf, but the identification remains provisional. The photograph records the arrangement without telling me everything living there or what each plant needs.

I remember mowing around this area for about two years. I had chosen to leave the growth beneath the structure while the place continued to be used for play. I had also noticed the plants before. What surprised me now was having to consider their future after the move.

### The circle, closer

The ring is roughly the size of the dome's footprint. The growth around its edge is low, maybe half a foot, narrow-leaved and dense enough to read as a band of green against the leaf litter. The center is different. A swing hung inside the dome, and two years of feet have worn the middle down. I called it a dry patch at first and then corrected myself while I was still looking: bare is what I can see. Whether it's dry is a claim about moisture, and I haven't put a hand to the soil.

I didn't move anything. I wanted a photograph of the arrangement before my own curiosity rearranged it.

The history matters here, and I got it partly wrong before I got it right. Nobody planted this as a patch. It also isn't ground that was simply left alone. I mowed around the dome on purpose, in part because the children wanted the plants under it kept, and they played there the whole time. So what I'm looking at is a place shaped by a play structure, a mower's path, a swing, small feet and a request... a used place with a history of selective restraint. Calling it untouched would flatter the story.

A word about names. In the recording I talk as though lilyturf and liriope were two candidates. They're the common and botanical names for the same genus. The question I left open on [November 7](2026-11-07%20-%20Whose%20Good%20Governs%20the%20Act.md) is still the open one: which species this is, and whether some of these clumps are a look-alike such as mondo grass. If it's liriope, it's most likely a planted ornamental that has wandered, and it may not be native here. I said out loud that caring for what's here doesn't require that it be what I'd have chosen. I still think that's right. It also doesn't settle what I should do if identification shows something that spreads at its neighbors' expense. The ivy on November 7 already taught me that a living plant's claim on my attention and a management decision are different questions.

I state a new commitment: leave this circle through winter. Concern for the plants and curiosity about what happens both enter that decision. Its future practice and effects will need their own record.

## Reflection

### Finishing the task

Moving the dome was a task I could finish in a morning. The good I hoped it might serve has a different measure. I cannot look at its new position and declare that the walnut has benefited. The tree continues through conditions and timescales that do not coincide with the end of my effort.

That difference is what I want “done isn't finished” to hold open. It does not mean that care can never succeed or that every purpose must continue indefinitely. It means that a successful movement and a good realized for another life are different things. Even a later change in the tree would need careful interpretation before I attributed it to this intervention.

I said this more boldly in the recording. I said that when an intention is for another living being, its fulfillment is kept by that being, and that I can complete the act without completing the intention. I want to keep the sentence and be careful with it. The walnut isn't withholding anything, and I'm not claiming that care can never arrive. What the sentence holds is an asymmetry of position. The moving was mine to do and mine to finish. Whether the ground over that root loosens, whether it mattered at all, belongs to a course of growth I don't run and can't hurry. I said it might show at next spring's leaf-out and then corrected myself: probably not then, probably years on, and even then tangled with drought, storms and everything else a tree lives through.

So there are at least three things where I'd been tempted to see one: the action, which is complete; the good it was for, which is open; and my knowledge of that good, which may stay partial even if the good is real.

### A beneficiary I hadn't made room for

The plant circle makes a second difference concrete. I acted with the walnut in mind and found myself considering another beneficiary. My general concern for the yard's life was already present. What became more specific was what I now intend this patch to be for: a place where these plants can continue while I learn more about them.

I don't want to make that a story of suddenly discovering life I had never seen. Previous seeing had not led me to consider what would happen here after relocation. The action made a familiar feature practically significant in a different way. My prepared questions helped me articulate that response. So did memory, curiosity and prior commitments.

### Seen, and not yet considered

Near the end of the sit I asked what I'd perceived that nothing I'd read predicted, and the answer that came was about me more than about the plants. I had seen this ring many times. I hadn't been blind to it. Yet across three days of planning the move I never once asked what would become of it. My care was aimed at the walnut's root, and that aim narrowed what counted as relevant. In a practical sense it did blind me, even while my eyes worked fine.

That's uncomfortable and useful. I've been arguing that another life's possible good can help specify what an act is for. This morning shows the other side of the same structure: a governing end also decides what drops out of consideration. An intention directed toward one beneficiary organizes a field of salience, and what falls outside it can be perfectly visible and still practically absent. The plants entered my purposes only when the completed action put a new question in front of me... what do I do with these now? Seeing was never the missing piece. Considering was.

I also need to correct something I said in the moment. I told the recorder that yesterday mowing meant keeping the yard even and safe to run on, and that this morning mowing this spot would mean mowing these plants. The second half is true. The first half tidies up the past. I had no settled plan to mow this circle yesterday; I'd been deliberately mowing around it for two years. What's new today is narrower and, I think, more interesting. The reason for leaving it has changed hands. It was left because a dome stood there and the children asked. Now the dome is gone, the old reason went with it, and I've had to decide whether the plants themselves are reason enough. I said they are, at least through the winter.

My reasons were mixed, and I said so at the time: the good of the soil, the good of these plants, a possible benefit to walnut roots that run under this ground, and plain curiosity about what happens. I also spoke of plant communication, intelligence and perhaps consciousness. Those are convictions and readings I carry with me. I didn't observe them in this ring this morning.

I gave myself a test as well. What would count against calling this a purpose I received from the place? If I'd have left the ring anyway because I like the look of a fairy ring in the yard, or because I didn't feel like mowing, or because it makes a good paragraph. I can't rule those out from inside the morning. I can check them against what I actually do when mowing starts again in March.

This is one way I am developing [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md). An end guides action, action alters a shared place, and attention to the affected lives can help specify a further end. The lives involved need not share my intentions. Their actual condition can still matter to what I intend next and to whether my response is adequate.

### Shelter, and what the word can carry

In the recording I said that my care for the walnut took these plants' shelter away, and that I had four goods in front of me that don't add up to one: the walnut's root, this patch, the children's play, a tidy yard. I called it a real case of goods in conflict. Listening back, that's ahead of the evidence.

There are several possible goods here, but I have not demonstrated a necessary conflict between them. The dome's former position belonged to a pattern of avoiding the mower. Its removal changes that arrangement without proving injury to the plants. Leaving the ring may be compatible with the move. Calling the old arrangement shelter is a reason to investigate what it did, not an established account of its biological effects.

What I can say is that our purposes had effects we didn't aim at. A structure put up for play took part in these plants' situation for two years, and an act of care for a tree changed that situation in a morning. For months I've been saying that other lives can take part in what I intend without intending anything alongside me. Here the traffic runs the other way too. What I intend takes part in other lives whether or not I've thought about them. If that's right, being answerable has to cover what my purposes do as well as what they aim at.

### Three sentences, said out loud

I ended by saying three sentences I might want in the paper, to hear whether I believed them.

First: when an intention is for another living being, I can finish the act and still not finish the intention; its fulfillment is kept in that life's own time. Standing there, this one felt closest to something I already knew from Stein's insistence that another's life is never given to me from the inside, and from Merleau-Ponty's gap. The research note below complicates how new it is.

Second: acting for one life can uncover another, and when it does, a new "for whom" can enter my ends that I didn't project and didn't choose. I said this one was good and that I had pushback of my own about it. The pushback is on the record above. "Uncover" overstates what happened to plants I'd already seen, and "didn't choose" sits oddly beside a decision I plainly made. What I didn't choose was the question.

Third: our purposes shelter and expose other lives without meaning to, so answerability has to cover what our ends do as well as what they aim at. I said this was the strongest of the three, and after the corrections I still think so. It asks the least of the evidence and the most of me.

[Remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md) belongs within this care. I can identify more, observe longer and make a definite commitment without becoming the author of these lives or their future.

## Research Connection

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) gives the distinction between intention and realized benefit a substantial predecessor. In *Finite and Eternal Being* V §§16–17, especially pp. 313–316 in Kurt F. Reinhardt's translation, she separates knowing a possible good from its actual realization in the beneficiary. Selected primary passages were checked after the sit. My developing extension concerns how the aftermath of one action can bring another beneficiary into the purpose of a subsequent act.

During the sit I reached for Aristotle's remark that "that for the sake of which" is twofold (τὸ οὗ ἕνεκα; *De Anima* II.4, 415b2–3, repeated at 415b20–21). On the long-standing reading he's distinguishing the aim of an act from the one for whom it's done, the οὗ from the ᾧ, and J. A. Smith's translation renders it that way. That gave me a compact way to say what changed at the circle: the aim of my yard work didn't need to change for a new "for whom" to enter it. Two cautions. The passage belongs to a discussion of nutrition and reproduction, and it makes no argument about what I owe a patch of groundcover. And the traditional reading has been challenged in recent scholarship, so I should cite it as the common interpretation and check the Greek against a critical edition before leaning on it in the paper.

[Husserl's](../concepts/Husserl.md) accounts of motivation and practical possibility remain a serious explanation of this sequence. Prepared language, memory and valued ends help make the response intelligible. The ecological proposal concerns the work done by independent living beneficiaries within that explanation; this case does not establish a new basic form of conscious activity.

I also owe Husserl a correction. In the recording I said he gives me fulfillment as the moment an empty intention meets what it meant. That's too quick, and the quickness flatters my own claim. His account of fulfillment is graded and temporal, and his lectures on willing describe an action as fulfilled progressively, phase by phase, as it's carried out. Discussions of those lectures in *The Routledge Handbook of Phenomenology of Agency* (2021) also distinguish the will at work in a present action from a resolve directed at a future end. That distinction fits this morning closely. Dragging the dome fulfilled the will to move it, in the doing. The resolve it served, an arrangement of this yard that's good for the walnut and still good for play, reaches past the action and remains unfulfilled. So "done isn't finished" is something Husserl can already say. What I'm adding, if I'm adding anything, concerns where the fulfilling evidence would have to come from: a continuing life that isn't mine. These are selected passages read after the sit, and I haven't worked through the lectures themselves.

The theological reflection turns to [Leviticus 25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus+25&version=NRSVUE). Land sabbath suggests a practiced limit on possession. My winter commitment is an analogy requiring further thought, not an instance of the chapter's agrarian institution. Its social exclusions also complicate any easy appeal to universal shared rest.

The detail that caught me is a single word. In the Greek of Leviticus 25:5, what the land produces during its sabbath is τὰ αὐτόματα ἀναβαίνοντα, the things that come up of themselves (αὐτόματος, "self-moved, of its own accord"; the phrase returns at 25:11). In the growing-seed parable I read here [yesterday](2026-11-10%20-%20A%20Future%20Not%20Mine%20Alone.md), Mark writes αὐτομάτη ἡ γῆ καρποφορεῖ, "of itself the earth bears fruit" (4:28). I checked both in online texts of the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament after the sit; they haven't been collated against the printed critical editions. I wondered aloud whether Mark had Leviticus in mind. I can't claim that. A shared word shows a shared vocabulary for growth that nobody engineered. Dependence would need its own evidence.

In the recording I called this circle a fallow we kept by accident. The phrase is half right. The rest wasn't accidental, since I chose not to mow there, and it wasn't rest from human presence, since the children were in it constantly. But Leviticus doesn't imagine an empty field either. During the land's sabbath its yield is food for the household and its slaves, hired and bound laborers, the livestock and the wild animals (25:6–7). The rest it describes is a limit on sowing, pruning and reaping as one's own, within a place that stays inhabited. Read that way, a ring of plants that came through two years of play is closer to the text than my word "accident" suggested. And the chapter's reason for the limit is a claim about ownership: "the land is mine," with the people as προσήλυτοι καὶ πάροικοι, strangers and sojourners (25:23; πάροικος, a resident without a citizen's title).

Moltmann is the reading-list voice I need here. In *God in Creation*, chapter XI, he treats the sabbath as the completion of creation and reads the sabbath year of Leviticus 25 as evidence that the feast belongs to the land and to the other creatures as well as to people. That gives the winter commitment a theological horizon larger than my yard without making my yard an instance of it. It also sharpens what's missing. Sabbath in that chapter is communal and scheduled. One person deciding to leave a circle unmowed is, at most, a private rehearsal of the posture.

For the *Ecology of the Cross*, I propose that participation in Christ can form communities willing to reconsider their purposes in light of affected lives. [Colossians 1:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1%3A15-20&version=NRSVUE), considered separately after the sit, supplies the Christological starting point. The extension into communal ecological responsibility requires argument and actual communal evidence. An imposed cost does not become redemptive because the person imposing it intended care.

I said one more thing at the tree that I want to keep on the record with its limits. I said a cruciform care doesn't wait until it can be innocent: it acts, it looks at what the act uncovered, and it stays. The staying is the part I can stand behind this morning. I moved the dome for one creature's sake, the move changed things for others, and I'm answerable for both. Whether "cruciform" is the right word for that answerability is a theological claim I haven't yet argued. It would need more than my willingness to keep looking, and it can't be allowed to turn somebody else's loss into my offering.

A congregation could begin with a concrete inquiry before changing its grounds: what is already living here, what practices have shaped the place, and whose needs have not yet entered the proposal? Answering would involve people who use and maintain the site as well as ecological investigation. That is a possible shared undertaking, not an outcome established by this backyard encounter.

The question I'd carry to such a conversation came out plainly at the tree. Communities set things down for good reasons: a parking lot, a fence, a playground, a mowing schedule. Things grow up under and around them that nobody decided on. Then something gets moved, for another good reason, and what it was covering comes to light. What's been living under the things we put down for other purposes, and who finds out when we move them? I can't answer that for any congregation from my backyard. I can listen for it.
