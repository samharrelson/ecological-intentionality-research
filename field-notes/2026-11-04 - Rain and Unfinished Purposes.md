*November 4, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 44 minutes recorded, including pauses*

## Prompt

> When care calls for better knowledge first, what would fulfill the inquiry? What would show that another life benefited?

## Field Notes

The drizzle is unexpected. Earlier sprinkles have become more sustained, and I hear the rain while looking at wet foliage. Leaves are falling. Yesterday I had wondered whether rain would bring more down; seeing rainfall and falling foliage together today doesn't establish why any particular piece detached.

The upper branches are increasingly exposed. Green and yellow remain lower in the canopy, with neighboring trees overlapping the view. Around the trunk, fallen yellow pieces lie among other leaves, acorns, and living groundcover. I recognize familiar forms, including plants I call violets and grasses, without having completed a species inventory.

Looking toward the oaks, the plants around their bases appear more varied to me. It is an informal comparison. I haven't counted species, compared equivalent areas, or established why the places differ.

The marks at the former swing attachment points are still visible in the rain. I can return to them with attention, but their appearance does not settle whether removal benefited the walnut.

## Reflection

I welcome the rain. That welcome tells me something about my response, while leaving its effects on the different lives here open. A shared condition need not serve every being in the same way.

The groundcover makes the question particular. What is growing beside the walnut? What does the litter do in this place? Are the plants benefiting from the arrangement, tolerating it, or present for reasons I haven't investigated?

I have usually left fallen material beneath the tree because I understood that as care. Today I state a more specific next intention: investigate the litter and the neighboring plants before deciding whether to change the practice. I do not make that management decision during the recording. Nor does stating an intention establish that the research has been done.

This is a small but useful movement. Care can give direction to an inquiry before it gives direction to an intervention. The inquiry must allow the existing practice to prove appropriate as well as mistaken. Otherwise I would be searching for a correction I had decided upon in advance.

## Research Connection

In the working essay [*Whose Good?*](https://carolinaecology.substack.com/p/whose-good), published on Carolina Ecology on November 4, I advance an affirmative account of [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md): purposes can become more determinate through answerability to independently living beings. This morning tests one practical extension of that claim, rather than presuming it is established. Today's sequence is observation, uncertainty about effects, a more particular question, and a stated commitment to investigate before choosing what to do. The plants' possible goods help specify what needs to be learned.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) gives this sequence a distinction that matters. In *Finite and Eternal Being*, V §16, pp. 313–314 in the 2002 Reinhardt translation, she distinguishes knowing a possible good from the actual events through which that good would be realized for its beneficiary. In §17, pp. 315–317, she distinguishes a being's own goodness from the goods others may contribute to it. She also places those differentiated goods within an eternal order, a metaphysical account that cannot be established from the plants growing together here.

Applied to this inquiry, learning more could fulfill my purpose as a researcher while leaving benefit to the walnut or its neighbors unestablished. The success of knowing and the success of caring need separate evidence. This is my application of Stein's distinction, not a botanical conclusion supplied by her text.

[Hal King's](../concepts/Hal%20King.md) account of situated plant empathy already makes inquiry into growing conditions part of practical engagement. These resources help construct the proposal and test its necessity. Ordinary deliberation and informed care might explain the whole sequence. The stronger claim that another organism participates in realizing an ongoing conscious act remains a further question.

The parallel *Ecology of the Cross* proposes that communion in Christ can transform communal ends when affected lives cease to be treated as expendable means. Its [provisional theological statement](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/5) requires distinct scriptural and institutional warrants. A congregation considering how to use its grounds would need to investigate whose good an activity serves, what it actually achieves, and who bears its costs. Welcoming rain, completing research, or adopting generous mission language cannot settle those questions.

## Question Carried Forward

**What must I learn before changing this practice? What would count as good for the lives concerned, beyond my satisfaction in understanding them?**
