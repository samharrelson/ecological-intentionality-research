*Sunday, November 8, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 53 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What has this relationship made possible for me to notice, understand or intend that wasn't available to me in the same way when I began? And what actually brought about those changes?

That is the positive question I want to begin with now. The work has become more than learning to distinguish a present appearance from a memory or inference, although that discipline still matters. I want to understand how repeated encounters with a continuing life have helped form the questions I can ask and the practical purposes I can undertake.

The walnut does not have to provide an insight this morning. An ordinary day could still belong to that history.

## Field Notes

The sky is blue and the sun is out. The air feels crisp and chilly, different from the damp cold of the preceding mornings. Light comes through a neighboring maple and across the yard toward the walnut. I see wrens, report crows passing overhead, and hear acorns falling from the oaks.

Fresh yellow leaves lie above brown and darker litter. Looking back toward the whole tree, I find long stretches of exposed upper branches with foliage remaining farther down. I recognize the difference through earlier visits and photographs. That history helps me see change without letting me witness all the events between the views.

## Reflection

### What returning has made possible

When I began this practice, I did not have the same questions about the ground beneath the walnut, the organisms that might use its leaves, or the placement of things near its roots. The tree had not been absent from my life. My ways of attending and deciding were less particular.

Its changing appearance matters to that development. So do the discipline of returning, the books I read, my earlier purposes, affection and memory. I don't think those mediations automatically compete with the relationship. Reading can be one way an encounter continues: something in the yard prompts a question, the question takes me to a source, and what I learn changes how I approach the yard again.

The walnut's contribution need not be unique to this walnut to be real. Other trees lose leaves and expose branches. This tree does so here, through a life with which I have this history of encounters. The inquiry concerns that particular contribution without making it the sole cause of what follows.

My positive proposal is that a sustained relationship with an independently living being can help form the repertoire of questions and purposes through which I act. The subject doing the noticing has a history, too. What I can notice as a practical problem has developed through the relation.

### The leaves: a plan with reported follow-through

The sequence of the leaf decision remains important. Questions about fallen material led to ecological reading. That reading contributed to my decision to leave whole leaves rather than repeat the planned mowing. By November 6 I was already reporting the revision; that morning's squirrels and falling leaf did not cause a decision made earlier.

Yesterday I also corrected the account of the original plan. I had been thinking about soil care and safe use of the yard as well as tidiness. The comparison was never simply between one selfish purpose and one ecological purpose. Several goods were already involved.

Today I can add something small but concrete: I have left the leaves uncollected since yesterday, as I intended. That is a report of follow-through over a bounded interval. It does not show which organisms are using the litter or establish that the decision has benefited them. I want those differences to remain visible: an intention, its implementation and its effects are three parts of the story.

### A new question beside an exposed root

Another practical possibility emerged more clearly during the recording. The climbing dome stands near an exposed walnut root. I estimated the visible root to be about a foot from the structure and began describing an intention to investigate moving the dome over winter.

I originally valued shade and a place for play. Those purposes still matter. Now I also ask what the placement means for the tree's continuing life. The root did not become important only because I learned a new philosophical word. Repeated attention and a growing concern for this particular tree have made the arrangement something I need to reconsider.

I have not established that the dome is damaging the root, moved the structure, or selected a verified better location. The alternatives I mention are possibilities. The next step is to record the current arrangement, its uses, what evidence is needed, and what could make me revise the plan. A location without visible roots would not by itself settle the condition of what lies below ground.

The practical purpose is becoming more specific before its outcome is known.

## Research Connection

The developing account of [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) begins with this history of formation. Independent living activity presents changing conditions. Repeated encounter gives those conditions a place among my expectations. Some become questions; inquiry brings possible beneficiaries and effects into view; deliberation reshapes a project; action or restraint creates another occasion for correction.

This is a process to explain and test. It is more definite than saying that the tree has become meaningful to me, but it does not isolate the walnut as the only cause of my changed habits. The task is to trace what the organism contributes alongside the practices and mediations through which I encounter it.

A meaningful artifact helps clarify the burden. A LEGO dinosaur can organize anticipation, attention and practical plans. Its parts have a material history and can resist what someone wants to do with them. So changing human intentions is not exclusive to living beings. The walnut's continuing organismic activity and possible good require a different account from the model's material properties, but that ontological difference does not automatically establish a new kind of conscious act. A photograph can also mediate concern for the real walnut: the medium and the intended beneficiary must be kept distinct.

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) already offers an account of developing familiarity and expectation as well as motivated practical response. The selected *Ideas II* §59 passage returned to in today's source check is a reminder that his subject has a history. [Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) supplies another substantial account through learning, memory and feedback. These are explanations with which the affirmative proposal must contend. Their possible success would not erase the recorded development of my purposes; it would change what further explanatory work ecological intentionality needs to do.

[Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) helps preserve another distinction. Missing botanical information is not the same as the other being's experience remaining unavailable to me. And knowing a possible good does not mean I have realized that good for its beneficiary.

I therefore need two tests. Evidence about the root, access or litter could justify changing my practical plan. Evidence that discriminates between philosophical accounts would have to establish something more about how the intention forms or is realized. A sound reason to move the dome would not, by itself, prove that Husserl's account fails.

### A separate question about common life

For the *Ecology of the Cross*, I stayed with [Philippians 2:1–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A1-11&version=NRSVUE). Its concern for communal regard leads into the account of Christ's self-emptying, death and exaltation. That movement gives the theological inquiry a particular starting point.

My constructive question is whether participation in Christ can form communities willing to reconsider the purposes governing their decisions. Extending that regard to more-than-human lives requires further theological warrants and ecological knowledge. A congregation's land-use decision would also involve access, work and the distribution of costs. Calling an imposed burden self-giving would not establish its faithfulness. This remains a proposed inquiry, not an accomplished congregational case.
