*Sunday, November 8, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 53 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What has this relationship made possible for me to notice, understand or intend that wasn't available to me in the same way when I began? And what actually brought about those changes?

That is the positive question I want to begin with now. The work has become more than learning to distinguish a present appearance from a memory or inference, although that discipline still matters. I want to understand how repeated encounters with a continuing life have helped form the questions I can ask and the practical purposes I can undertake.

The walnut does not have to provide an insight this morning. An ordinary day could still belong to that history.

## Field Notes

The sky is blue and the sun is out. The air feels crisp and chilly, different from the damp cold of the preceding mornings. Light comes through a neighboring maple and across the yard toward the walnut. I see wrens, report crows passing overhead, and hear acorns falling from the oaks.

Fresh yellow leaves lie above brown and darker litter. Looking back toward the whole tree, I find long stretches of exposed upper branches with foliage remaining farther down. I recognize the difference through earlier visits and photographs. That history helps me see change without letting me witness all the events between the views.

## Reflection

### What returning has made possible

When I began this practice, I did not have the same questions about the ground beneath the walnut, the organisms that might use its leaves, or the placement of things near its roots. The tree had not been absent from my life. My ways of attending and deciding were less particular.

Its changing appearance matters to that development. So do the discipline of returning, the books I read, my earlier purposes, affection and memory. I don't think those mediations automatically compete with the relationship. Reading can be one way an encounter continues: something in the yard prompts a question, the question takes me to a source, and what I learn changes how I approach the yard again.

The walnut's contribution need not be unique to this walnut to be real. Other trees lose leaves and expose branches. This tree does so here, through a life with which I have this history of encounters. The inquiry concerns that particular contribution without making it the sole cause of what follows.

My positive proposal is that a sustained relationship with an independently living being can help form the repertoire of questions and purposes through which I act. The subject doing the noticing has a history, too. What I can notice as a practical problem has developed through the relation.

### What happens when a book interrupts me?

A second, quite different kind of interruption happened yesterday. I picked up Joseph Campbell's *The Flight of the Wild Gander* again and found myself reading about the Brothers Grimm, Clemens Brentano and Achim von Arnim. A name leapt off the page. Was this Clemens Brentano connected to the Franz Brentano who has been following me through so much of the intentionality question?

I went looking. The family connection really is there: the Romantic poet Clemens was Franz Brentano's uncle. It was one of those delightful moments when something I was reading for pleasure unexpectedly returned me to an academic question. I hadn't gone looking for another phenomenological connection. The association redirected what I wanted to investigate.

But that episode is also a helpful check on the philosophical story I might tell about the walnut. A text can change the trajectory of my attention. A remembered name can become suddenly salient. A conversation with another person, a book, or an artifact can alter what I expect to find next. I wouldn't want to attribute that little discovery directly to the walnut's biological activity. The exact Campbell passage and page still need checking in the physical copy, so I'm treating this as an episode of my own reading rather than offering Campbell as a verified philosophical authority here.

What, then, is the difference between the formative history of a text and that of a living organism? I can't answer that by saying only living things surprise me. They clearly don't. Nor can I say that reading is merely a distraction from the living relation. When a book helps me understand what might be happening in the leaf litter, the reading may become one of the ways I respond to this actual place. The question is whether the learning remains answerable to the specific lives here, rather than making them illustrations of something I read elsewhere.

I also found myself thinking about Ursula K. Le Guin's *Always Coming Home*. It's a fictional work that asks me to inhabit forms of cultural attention very different from my own. It can unsettle what I take for granted about place, use and naming. But fiction is not an ethnographic report about an actual Indigenous people, any more than a fictional dinosaur is a living animal. Its imaginative power gives me questions to carry back to the world, not evidence that those questions have been answered.

These interruptions matter because the history I'm trying to understand extends well beyond my morning seat. Yet the distinctions matter just as much: a work of fiction, a historical book, another person's question, and a growing walnut do not contribute to my intentional life in exactly the same way.

### The leaves: a plan with reported follow-through

The sequence of the leaf decision remains important. Questions about fallen material led to ecological reading. That reading contributed to my decision to leave whole leaves rather than repeat the planned mowing. By November 6 I was already reporting the revision; that morning's squirrels and falling leaf did not cause a decision made earlier.

Yesterday I also corrected the account of the original plan. I had been thinking about soil care and safe use of the yard as well as tidiness. The comparison was never simply between one selfish purpose and one ecological purpose. Several goods were already involved.

Today I can add something small but concrete: I have left the leaves uncollected since yesterday, as I intended. That is a report of follow-through over a bounded interval. It does not show which organisms are using the litter or establish that the decision has benefited them. I want those differences to remain visible: an intention, its implementation and its effects are three parts of the story.

### A new question beside an exposed root

Another practical possibility emerged more clearly during the recording. The climbing dome stands near an exposed walnut root. I estimated the visible root to be about a foot from the structure and began describing an intention to investigate moving the dome over winter.

I originally valued shade and a place for play. Those purposes still matter. Now I also ask what the placement means for the tree's continuing life. The root did not become important only because I learned a new philosophical word. Repeated attention and a growing concern for this particular tree have made the arrangement something I need to reconsider.

I have not established that the dome is damaging the root, moved the structure, or selected a verified better location. The alternatives I mention are possibilities. The next step is to record the current arrangement, its uses, what evidence is needed, and what could make me revise the plan. A location without visible roots would not by itself settle the condition of what lies below ground.

The practical purpose is becoming more specific before its outcome is known.

### The measure of care cannot be my intention alone

I keep coming back to the difference between having a purpose and realizing a good. If I decide to move the dome, I could be acting out of concern for the walnut while still making an ecologically uninformed choice. The new position could be worse for the tree, less useful for the children, or inconvenient for ordinary movement through the yard. A place where I see no exposed roots is not necessarily free of roots beneath the ground.

Likewise, retaining whole leaves may be a reasonable response to what I've learned about overwintering habitat. But I don't yet know which creatures are in this litter, how its particular distribution matters to them, or whether an arrangement that helps one group creates a problem for another. I can document the action or the restraint. I cannot infer its full consequences from my reason for choosing it.

There is a useful tension here. If I insist on waiting until I have complete knowledge, I might never act. If I treat every compassionate purpose as sufficient, I can easily mistake goodwill for adequate care. The discipline I'm after is something between those temptations: make a responsible decision on the evidence available, say what is uncertain, and keep the decision open to further correction.

Stein helps me articulate the tension between a possible good and its actual realization. But there is no shortcut from that philosophical distinction to a management instruction for this particular yard. Botanical evidence, local conditions, the uses of the space and, sometimes, knowledgeable advice from others still matter.

Today's new finding is therefore modest but substantive. I have reported doing what I said I intended concerning the leaves, and I have named another particular situation in which the tree's possible good may change a future practical decision. In one case there is an interval of enacted restraint. In the other there is a prospective inquiry and a possible move. I would obscure both if I described them merely as evidence that I am becoming a more ecological person.

## Research Connection

The developing account of [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) begins with this history of formation. Independent living activity presents changing conditions. Repeated encounter gives those conditions a place among my expectations. Some become questions; inquiry brings possible beneficiaries and effects into view; deliberation reshapes a project; action or restraint creates another occasion for correction.

This is a process to explain and test. It is more definite than saying that the tree has become meaningful to me, but it does not isolate the walnut as the only cause of my changed habits. The task is to trace what the organism contributes alongside the practices and mediations through which I encounter it.

A meaningful artifact helps clarify the burden. A LEGO dinosaur can organize anticipation, attention and practical plans. Its parts have a material history and can resist what someone wants to do with them. So changing human intentions is not exclusive to living beings. The walnut's continuing organismic activity and possible good require a different account from the model's material properties, but that ontological difference does not automatically establish a new kind of conscious act. A photograph can also mediate concern for the real walnut: the medium and the intended beneficiary must be kept distinct.

I hadn't expected an ordinary trip to a LEGO resale shop to become part of the next morning's philosophical reflection. An older *Indominus Rex Breakout* set had become the subject of considerable anticipation. In the *Jurassic World* story, the engineered Indominus Rex is imagined as a living creature that refuses to remain contained by the designs of its makers. The LEGO set is a material model of that fictional creature, carrying its own history as an artifact and its own significance for the person who cares about it.

That's a useful set of distinctions. The dinosaur of the film is fictional, even though the story imagines an organism with capacities, vulnerabilities and activity of its own. The LEGO dinosaur is real, but not biologically alive. Its pieces can break, resist assembly, or wear over time; those are actual material constraints. None of them makes it an independently self-maintaining organism. And the meaning of the set to someone who treasures it is real, even though the represented dinosaur isn't.

This comparison defeats an easy version of my argument. I can't say the walnut is different simply because an encounter with it can change someone's intentions. The LEGO set already shows that something not alive can do that. A piece of music, an old book or an Assyrian relief can do so too. What makes the walnut's contribution different, if anything, must concern the kind of life and organization I encounter, not only the power of its significance for me.

The living walnut is developing, maintaining and responding through biological processes that continue beyond my attention. The conditions under which it could flourish or suffer aren't identical with the purposes I assign to it. But to move from that difference to a claim about a new structure of *my intending* would take another argument. I need to keep open the possibility that what differs is the ontology of the being addressed and the norm of adequate care, while phenomenology already has the resources to describe my motives and habits.

The comparison also exposes something about this public research itself. A reader may encounter this account through a screen, an image, or a link, without standing beside the walnut. That mediated encounter might still become an intention to learn about a living place. It would be a mistake to insist that the medium must be alive for the referent to matter. But it would be another mistake to treat a moving description as if it guarantees an adequate understanding of the organism described.

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) already offers an account of developing familiarity and expectation as well as motivated practical response. The selected *Ideas II* §59 passage returned to in today's source check is a reminder that his subject has a history. [Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) supplies another substantial account through learning, memory and feedback. These are explanations with which the affirmative proposal must contend. Their possible success would not erase the recorded development of my purposes; it would change what further explanatory work ecological intentionality needs to do.

[Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) helps preserve another distinction. Missing botanical information is not the same as the other being's experience remaining unavailable to me. And knowing a possible good does not mean I have realized that good for its beneficiary.

I therefore need two tests. Evidence about the root, access or litter could justify changing my practical plan. Evidence that discriminates between philosophical accounts would have to establish something more about how the intention forms or is realized. A sound reason to move the dome would not, by itself, prove that Husserl's account fails.

### A separate question about common life

For the *Ecology of the Cross*, I stayed with [Philippians 2:1–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A1-11&version=NRSVUE). Its concern for communal regard leads into the account of Christ's self-emptying, death and exaltation. That movement gives the theological inquiry a particular starting point.

My constructive question is whether participation in Christ can form communities willing to reconsider the purposes governing their decisions. Extending that regard to more-than-human lives requires further theological warrants and ecological knowledge. A congregation's land-use decision would also involve access, work and the distribution of costs. Calling an imposed burden self-giving would not establish its faithfulness. This remains a proposed inquiry, not an accomplished congregational case.

I want to hold the actual movement of Philippians 2 in view. Paul's exhortation to consider the interests of others doesn't appear as an isolated piece of moral advice. It moves into an account of Christ's refusal to treat status as something to exploit for advantage, his taking the form of a servant, and a form of obedience that extends to death. The passage then turns toward exaltation and confession of Christ as Lord. I cannot reduce those claims to a generalized lesson about being less selfish without losing their Christological particularity.

Still, I find something constructive in asking whether the mind of Christ changes how a community understands the ends it is pursuing. We can profess a broad purpose such as welcome or care while particular decisions are governed by a much narrower collection of habits. That isn't necessarily hypocrisy. A building has to be maintained, people need safe routes, money has to be accounted for, and someone has to do the work. I have just spent two mornings correcting my own tendency to describe ordinary considerations like tidiness and access as though they were necessarily opposed to ecological life.

The harder question is what happens when a community discovers that its original practical description left someone, or some life, out of account. Could shared participation in Christ make that discovery a reason to reconsider not only the means but the very purpose of an action? I think that is a worthwhile theological proposition. It would need to be argued through Scripture, theological history, and the actual circumstances of a community rather than inferred from my personal experience with one walnut.

There's also a danger I want to resist. A congregation can call something sacrifice while asking less powerful people to bear the costs. A property decision can be presented as stewardship while erasing the plants, creatures or histories already there. Neither becomes good because I give it Christian language. The cross does not provide a sacred justification for imposing harm on others, and resurrection does not tell me that environmental losses will somehow be made harmless.

This means that the Ecology of the Cross, if it is going to be an affirmative theology of creaturely participation, must keep asking who participates, who is affected, whose account of the situation counts, and what kind of evidence could correct the community's understanding. The walnut cannot answer every theological question, but the discipline of returning to a living place may help expose the responsibilities involved in asking them.
