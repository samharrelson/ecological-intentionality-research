*Monday, November 9, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 24 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What am I directed toward, and what does this independently living relation contribute to the direction of that intention?

I came to the walnut with a working proposal: consciousness can receive direction through relationships with lives and places it does not author. This morning I wanted to describe one actual intention through which to develop that thought.

## Field Notes

The sky is blue, sunlight comes through the walnut, and the cold is something I feel on my skin. There is little breeze. Bird calls mingle with construction noise and passing cars.

The upper branches are almost bare. Yellow foliage remains lower down, while the ground holds brown fallen material with fresher yellow pieces above it. During the sit I watch a couple of leaves detach and fall. Yesterday afternoon's unexpectedly open canopy remains in memory beside what I see now.

*Photograph available on the [illustrated field-notes site](https://ecologicalintentionality.org/sits/2026-11-09).*

*November 9. Exposed upper branches, remaining yellow foliage, sunlight through a lower branch and fallen material beneath the walnut. The climbing dome appears at the right; the photograph records placement, not its effects on the tree.*

The dome stands near a visible root. I have tried moving it enough to think I could relocate it, and I have a possible alternative position in mind. I still have not completed that relocation or established that the present arrangement harms the walnut.

Later, I find myself looking at the bark. The surface holds my attention while the processes I understand to continue within the tree remain unavailable to this immediate view.

There are other things in this morning's field besides the tree. I hear calls that I take to be thrashers and Carolina wrens, nearby construction, and traffic. I notice how the position of the morning sunlight seems different from other mornings when I've stood in this spot. Part of that comparison comes from memory, not from measuring the sun's position today. The sky and the sounds matter, too. This is an ordinary yard amid other human projects, not an isolated philosophical experiment.

### A practical trial before I began speaking

I should be clear about the sequence of the dome decision. Yesterday I was already considering moving the climbing dome, partly because I had begun noticing a root near it. Overnight I continued thinking about it, and **before this morning's recording I tried the structure enough to see whether I could move it myself**. That trial made relocation feel possible. It did not establish that I had found the right new place or that the tree needed the structure moved.

I also took a photograph of the root and dome together. I'm considering a position perhaps ten feet farther toward the middle of the yard. That distance is my provisional idea, not a conclusion from a root-health assessment. The dome has been a place of shade and play for the children, so this isn't a situation in which the human purpose was worthless and the walnut's purpose was automatically right. Several goods belong to the arrangement.

I can now say more confidently that moving the dome is something I think I **can** do. Whether moving it is something I **ought** to do, and what would actually serve the walnut's continuing life, are separate questions. Nothing in this recording establishes that I carried out the move.

## Reflection

### What the intended move is for

The dome was placed here for shade and play. Those remain good purposes. Now the walnut's possible good enters my intention for the arrangement as well. I want continued use of the space to be answerable to this tree's life.

That gives the philosophical proposal something specific to explain. The walnut is more than the object I happen to look at while considering an already fixed project. My relation with it may help specify the future I am trying to bring about: what I hope to sustain, whose good matters within that hope, and what could make the proposed action a mistake.

The relation includes returning, remembering, reading and thinking overnight. Those mediations do not make the concern unreal. They do mean that I cannot attribute the whole intention to an isolated moment of seeing a root.

Trying the dome adds another element. A possible project becomes something I think I can undertake. But the trial answers a question about ability. It does not establish that relocation would help the tree, or show that my purpose changed while I tested the structure.

I can therefore carry a definite intention without making movement itself the measure of success. If better evidence favored leaving the dome where it is, care for the walnut could require a different means. If I remained determined to move it regardless of what I learned, I would need to reconsider my account of what the action was for.

### The ability isn't the purpose

There's a distinction here that feels small until I try to describe the conscious act itself. I didn't go outside this morning with the sole aim of discovering whether a metal and plastic structure could be moved. I went out with a concern about the walnut and about the family's use of this space. When I tested the dome, I learned something about the means available to me. But my intention to do something for the walnut's possible good was already in play.

So what am I actually directed toward? Not only a dome, a root or a position ten feet away. I'm directed toward a possible future arrangement in which play continues and I have reason to think I'm not imposing unnecessary costs on this living tree. The walnut's good is something I mean within the project, although I haven't yet established what that good requires in this particular situation.

This is what I wanted to explore last night with the language of *received directedness*. Perhaps another life can participate in specifying what my intention is for, rather than being added to a project whose purpose was fixed in advance.

I need to be able to say what could prove my choice mistaken. If I learn that the dome's present position is harmless and that moving it would damage something else, the same concern for the walnut might require me to do nothing. If I insist on moving it no matter what I learn, I would have good reason to reconsider whether this is genuinely a walnut-answerable intention. Caring about another being doesn't mean I get to make my proposal immune to that being's reality.

This doesn't establish a new category of intentionality. It gives me a particular act to describe: an end that I can name, a bodily possibility I tested, an action still projected, and a standard of success that is not mine to declare fulfilled.

### The tree doesn't deliver a parable

About halfway through the sit, I found myself comparing this relationship to the way a parable can unsettle a listener. I love the Good Samaritan and the prodigal son partly because they don't function merely as flat instructions. You can return to the same story and be challenged in a different way because of who you are and what you're bringing to it.

I don't mean that the walnut is telling me a story or preaching at me. It isn't secretly giving me a command about my yard. Rather, the relationship keeps disturbing the questions I thought I had already settled. I'm out here with one purpose, and something about this actual place can make me ask what that purpose is for. The parable is my interpretive comparison, not evidence that the tree has composed one.

That distinction matters. A metaphor may help me notice a genuine interruption, but it doesn't prove what produced the interruption or what another being intends.

### Nearness that leaves a remainder

Looking at bark is another act. Here I am trying to understand something about a life whose surface is available and whose continuing processes exceed what I can watch this morning.

Some of what I cannot see could become better known through biological inquiry. That is different from claiming access to the tree's experience. And neither more information nor greater familiarity would make its life coincide with mine.

This is what I mean by intimacy with [remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md): a relation can become more particular and more consequential while the other remains independent. Its possible good can matter within my intention without being exhausted by what I currently believe that good to be.

### A stronger claim I still want to investigate

I also said something more speculative aloud today. I suspect that trees and other living beings, and perhaps the material relationships of rivers, rocks and soil, involve forms of conscious activity that we humans are inclined to overlook. That's a metaphysical position I want to take seriously enough to investigate, not something I observed directly by standing beneath a black walnut for twenty-four minutes.

I don't mean that a rock, a river, a walnut and a human being necessarily possess the same sort of inner life. Some of these are organisms; some are material formations or relations that include living and nonliving processes. Nor does reciprocal influence by itself prove that the other side of the relationship intends me as I intend it.

What today's encounter can establish is narrower, and perhaps more useful for the present paper. I'm involved with a living being whose ongoing activity I don't author. My conscious project is beginning to include what I understand as that being's possible good. I'm trying to discover what it would mean for that good to participate in the direction of the intention, not merely appear later as an external ethical test.

This is precisely where **intimacy with remainder** becomes something more than a description of uncertainty. The closer I become to the walnut and the more concrete my purposes become, the more important it is that the tree is not exhausted by my description of it. Some of what I don't know is researchable. Otherness doesn't disappear when those facts become clearer.

## Research Connection

The positive account of [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) now has three distinguishable moments to work with: a relation helps specify an intended end; practical exploration makes a means available; further encounter and inquiry can correct the project. These moments overlap in experience, but their evidence differs. A reported trial is not a completed relocation, and a completed relocation would still require an account of its consequences.

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) helps make the middle moment precise. In a selected passage from *Ideas II* §60, practical possibility concerns what the subject experiences as within their capability. That offers a strong account of why trying the dome changes what I can envisage doing. His existing account of motivation also challenges any suggestion that phenomenology knows only a passive spectator. The unresolved question is what the living relation contributes to specifying the end of this present intention. Today's case develops that question without demonstrating a new basic structure of consciousness.

The congregational question begins from a different warrant. [Psalm 104:10–24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+104%3A10-24&version=NRSVUE) places human activity among springs, trees, habitats and other creatures' rhythms. I read this as an invitation to understand human purposes within a world whose provision exceeds our use of it.

For the *Ecology of the Cross*, I propose that participation in Christ can form a communal “we” whose mission remains receptive to fellow creatures. [Colossians 1:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1%3A15-20&version=NRSVUE) connects creation, the church, resurrection and reconciliation through the cross. Extending that confession into ecological communal discernment is an argument to develop, not a decision procedure stated in the passage.

A congregation might begin a grounds decision by asking how to maintain its property, then discover reasons to reconsider what that property serves. A real case would need ecological knowledge, the history of the place, participation from affected people and evidence of what the community actually changes. It would also have to ask who bears the costs. Calling a decision sacrificial cannot make imposed injury good.

### A communal direction that can be received

This is something I don't want to put off until a final application section of the dissertation. I've spent much of my adult life among congregations and people trying to work out what a community is supposed to do. Shared purposes can genuinely form us, but they can also narrow what we regard as possible or important. We can become very good at preserving a mission without asking whether our understanding of it needs to change.

I keep wondering whether the actual lives of a congregation's place can enter that shared directedness. Not as a nature-themed addition to an established ministry, but as part of what helps a community understand its own purposes differently. We might become capable of consciously intending a future together that we weren't able to articulate before a relationship with that place began to change us.

Psalm 104 is interesting to me because its creaturely world is not arranged around a church's usefulness. In the passage I considered this morning, springs give drink to wild animals, birds have nesting places, trees are watered, mountain goats have their heights, and rocks shelter other creatures. Human work appears within that differentiated world in verse 23, not as the exclusive reason the world exists. That's not yet an ecclesiology, but it is a compelling place to ask about receiving a purpose within a creation that exceeds our purposes.

For the Ecology of the Cross, I want something more particular than a call to appreciate interconnection. What does participation in Christ do to a community's shared direction? How might that participation form a *we* whose understanding of mission can still be corrected by lives we don't own and can't fully represent? Those questions require Christian theological argument and real congregational practice. Today's walnut encounter gives me a way to state them, not the authority to announce that a church has already answered them.

### What I can and cannot claim from today

This morning gives me a **reported practical trial** and a **presently stated intention**. It doesn't give me a completed relocation, a diagnosis of root injury, an ecological success measurement, evidence that the walnut consciously intended anything toward me, or proof that Husserl's account has failed.

It also gives me two different acts that I don't want to combine. Testing a dome concerns a possible action and its beneficiary. Looking at bark concerns knowing and encountering a continuing life that exceeds my present view. Both matter to the larger question of how directed consciousness can be received and redirected, but they aren't the same event.

The script I brought outside supplied some of today's language in advance. Books, previous encounters, family purposes and last night's writing are all part of the mediation. The distinctive event in the record is that I had already tried the dome and could now describe a more actionable intention. That may be modest, but it's enough to carry into the paper.

## Sources and limits

The selected textual comparison is **Edmund Husserl, *Ideas II*, §60(a)**, in the Richard Rojcewicz and André Schuwer translation (Kluwer, 1989), on experienced practical possibility and the "I can." This is a section-level reference, not a newly page-checked direct quotation. **James Jardine, *Empathy, Embodiment, and the Person* (Springer, 2022), especially chapter 4, §4.4, and chapter 6, §§6.1.2 and 6.3**, provides further resources for how worldly circumstances motivate valuation, action, agency and habituality. I'm treating those as close-reading targets for the constructive draft, not as arguments I have already settled.
