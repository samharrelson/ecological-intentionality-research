*September 29, 2026 · Black walnut · 25 minutes*

## Prompt

> When does what is outside the walnut become part of the walnut?

## Field Notes

After several days of looking upward, I looked down. The trunk widened into the ground. Large roots appeared at the surface and disappeared beneath dry soil. Plants grew around the base. More leaves and walnuts had fallen, while other walnuts remained in the canopy. Ants moved across the bark, and a few flying insects passed nearby.

What was visible was modest: bark, exposed root, ground, surrounding vegetation, fallen material, sunlight, shade, and movement. Root uptake, metabolism, cell formation, and any particular fungal relation were not visible. Those processes may be scientifically investigable, but their presence and character cannot be established from this photograph alone.

## Reflection

At first the distinction seemed easy: walnut here, environment there. The visible roots immediately complicated it. The tree remains this tree while water, gases, minerals, and other matter enter, are transformed, and may become integrated into its living body. Leaves, fruit, gases, and dead tissue also leave. The organism persists without retaining exactly the same matter.

That makes Vegetal Encounter a question of crossing as well as appearance. A power line or neighboring tree can condition the walnut without becoming part of its organism. Material taken up through roots or leaves can cross a boundary and become incorporated into one life. **External conditioning is not incorporation.**

The movement itself carries no visible norm. An autumn leaf, fallen fruit, storm-torn limb, pruned branch, absorbed nutrient, toxin, pathogen, drought, and excessive water do not mean the same thing. **Exchange is not flourishing. Material openness is not ecological harmony.** Remainder includes the difference between a process being scientifically investigable and its being given here, as well as the unresolved norm by which a crossing counts as sustaining, developmental, reproductive, or harmful.

The better formulation is therefore: **a boundary need not be a wall, and permeability need not mean indistinction.** The walnut depends without dissolving into everything that sustains it. Its integrity does not require isolation or material closure.

## Research Connection

Husserl helps distinguish hidden profiles from explanatory processes. The visible trunk and exposed roots can support an anticipation of below-ground continuation, and familiarity with trees shapes what I expect beyond the visible surface. But the determinate architecture of this root system is not thereby given. Horizonality also does not explain metabolism or how absorbed matter becomes integrated into one organism. Those questions call for biology and, at another level, metaphysics.

Edith Stein supplies the strongest metaphysical account in *Finite and Eternal Being*. Around p. 247, plant form can appropriate material from beyond the limits of the existing whole, transform it, and incorporate it into that whole. Around p. 253, the parts belong as members or organs of one living organism; around pp. 368–369, living form integrates changing material into an articulated unity. Organismic unity therefore persists through material change rather than through material sameness. This is Stein’s metaphysical claim, not another feature visible beside bark and soil.

Stein’s account also creates a theological temptation that must be resisted. This walnut depends on water, light, gases, minerals, and other conditions beyond itself. Around p. 61, Stein contrasts finite being, which does not possess its being and receives it anew, with eternal being as full possession of being. Those claims may eventually become analogically or formatively illuminating, but **material reception is not received *esse***. Botanical uptake cannot serve as empirical evidence for a metaphysics of creaturely dependence.

Robin Wall Kimmerer challenges the grammar of isolated individual plus available resource. “The Gift of Strawberries,” especially pp. 29–30, is useful here because gift names a transformed relation and mode of reception rather than a biological description of transfer. That does not license calling nutrient uptake *gift* or every material exchange *reciprocity*. Those ethical terms still have to earn their names.

Vanessa Watts makes “environment” less secure as a neutral category surrounding an already complete organism. Her specifically Haudenosaunee and Anishnaabe account of Place-Thought does not require organism and soil to become indistinguishable. It challenges an architecture in which histories, agencies, relations, and obligations are treated as secondary context.

The implication for Place and Agency and Ecological Intentionality is methodological. Attention describes the visible boundary; biology investigates crossing and regulation; metaphysics asks what unity persists through change; ethics distinguishes benefit from harm; theology must justify its analogies. Ecological inquiry becomes more adequate when none of these modes claims to have completed the work of the others.

## Question Carried Forward

**What differentiates external conditioning, material crossing, incorporation, and membership in a living whole? What norm distinguishes sustaining exchange from injury or impairment?**
