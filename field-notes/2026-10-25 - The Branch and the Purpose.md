*October 25, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 28½ minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What does another being's ongoing life contribute to the formation of a purpose it does not share?

## Field Notes

Bright light catches the walnut's crown. Yellow and green leaves stand against blue sky; fallen leaves and acorns lie below. The swing is still hanging, its straps and rings visible above the circular seat. Yesterday's intention to take it down has not become a completed act. The children were still playing on it last night while we gathered around the fire pit and made s'mores. When I mentioned taking it down, they weren't quite ready to let it go. Their wishes are part of what I remember this morning, but I can't take them as a measure of the walnut's good.

*October 25, 2026. Morning light through the walnut's branching crown. The neighboring canopy shares the view without disclosing the full relationship between the trees.*

Looking up, I notice more yellow than green. Yet I cannot easily separate this morning's appearance from my memory of yesterday. A little later, a gentle breeze moves the smaller branches, and the light has changed again.

## Reflection

The swing makes a human purpose easy to recognize. The branch has a different history, belonging to a life that did not develop in order to support that purpose. My concern about the arrangement is a third activity, still unfinished.

These histories meet without becoming interchangeable. Remembered use helps shape what the swing means to me. The tree's condition matters to whether an intervention would help it. Neither consideration tells me that I know the walnut's good simply by looking, or that the tree shares my intention.

The overlapping canopies offer another version of this difficulty. I see leaves and branches occupying the same view. Growth over time, competition, and the condition of each tree require more than that view can provide. Their relation may matter greatly while remaining only partly understood.

## Research Connection

[Raymond Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) gives this comparison a useful complication. In *Neofinalism*, he describes organic activity extending into manufactured tools and machines, which can be organized by form without being living organs (trans. Alyosha Edlebi, 2016, pp. 36–37). He also distinguishes an organ from a tool constructed and inspected from outside (p. 44). An attachment can belong to an organized practice without becoming part of the tree's living body. Ruyer's further account of living form and the distinction between organized human activity and an organ are philosophical claims, not a diagnosis of this branch.

Husserl's *Ideas II* (trans. Richard Rojcewicz and André Schuwer, 1989, §§49–51, 55–56) also describes a familiar practical world in which memory, valuation, and motivation can alter what I intend. The changed purpose alone doesn't establish a newly constituted consciousness.

That leaves a question for [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md): how does a purpose develop when it relies on another independently living being? The walnut is more than the meaning I assign to it, but that does not establish that its activity constitutes my conscious intention. It may shape what I learn, what I notice, and what I can successfully do. A stronger account must explain what the ongoing relation contributes to intending itself.

This morning keeps that positive proposal concrete. A purpose formed in relation to another life need not be a purpose shared by that life. Understanding the difference is part of learning what participation could mean.

## Question Carried Forward

**How could I distinguish another being's contribution to my intending from what I believe about it and what would make my care succeed?**
