*October 28, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 33 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What can I know about an act of care after I have already acted?

## Field Notes

Before recording, I noticed a large branch hanging in a position I didn't remember from yesterday. The wood looked damaged. I took a photograph, examined it from the ground, and pulled it down. It now lies near the compost pile.

*Photograph available on the [illustrated field-notes site](https://ecologicalintentionality.org/sits/2026-10-28).*

*October 28, before removal. A hanging section and exposed-looking wood among green and yellow leaves. The photograph does not establish when the damage occurred or whether the tissues were alive.*

Looking up afterward, I cannot identify exactly where the branch broke from. The canopy is still mostly green. Yellow leaves fall every few minutes, bright in the sun. There is a little wind, and the morning grows warmer.

During a pause, another feature catches my attention: a small protrusion high on the trunk, on a side I look at less often. It is beyond my reach. I can see its shape without knowing what produced it.

## Reflection

I came prepared to think about the removed branch. The unfamiliar feature brought me back to the tree that remains. I found myself wondering whether it was ordinary growth, an old injury, or something else—and whether I would have asked in quite this way before spending so much time here.

Familiarity seems to change what I notice and what becomes a question of care. It does not tell me that a feature is new, or that the tree needs an intervention. This is one form of [remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md): a feature becomes visible while its history stays open to inquiry.

The branch removal had several possible purposes. I wanted the yard to be safe for the children, I wanted the space to be orderly, and I understood myself as caring for the walnut. Those aims can overlap without meaning the same thing, or serving the same good. I did not see the original break, test the tissues, or establish that removal benefited the tree. I can describe the action more confidently than its adequacy.

## Research Connection

[Raymond Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) helps sharpen the difference between a visible arrangement and a living organization. His *Neofinalism* treats form as continuing activity and considers how component individualities belong to larger wholes (trans. Alyosha Edlebi, 2016, ch. 14, pp. 143, 147, 150–151). That makes the branch's membership a question about more than where it hangs. His metaphysics cannot diagnose this particular branch from its photograph.

For [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md), I want to investigate how an intention of care develops through repeated encounters with a life I do not control. The protrusion gives that inquiry an ordinary example: prior concern helps shape what attracts attention next. Yet learning and motivated attention may already explain that change. An artificial hanging limb could also prompt removal, and a mistaken judgment about a living branch could prompt sincere concern.

The inquiry needs to distinguish why I act, whether the action benefits another being, and how the intention itself takes shape. Neither good intentions nor mixed motives settle all three questions.

## Question Carried Forward

**How does another being's continuing life shape an intention of care—and what evidence would distinguish that contribution from familiarity, belief, and the conditions of successful action?**
