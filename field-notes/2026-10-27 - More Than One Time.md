## Prompt

> Can I attend to differently unfolding lives without pretending to experience another being's time from within?

## Field Notes

The forecast said fog. Looking into the branches, I find clear air instead. Leaves and grass are damp, as is the ground around the walnut. Green and yellow remain together in the canopy. Below, fallen leaves lie around roots that emerge briefly before returning underground.

## Reflection

The roots bring summer's fuller grass to mind. The leaves recall September and suggest the bare branches I expect in winter. Yet memory and anticipation do not put those seasons before my eyes. What appears now is this damp ground and this changing light.

I've been taking photographs of this tree from much the same place, morning after morning, partly because I'm curious what changes between them. The photographs help, but they cannot show me everything that happened in between. Nor do they let me inhabit the walnut's own life from within. [Attention](../concepts/Attention.md) can deepen without turning another creature's history into my possession.

What surprised me as I spoke today was where the question led. I started with the soil around the walnut and wound up thinking about the bacteria living in and on my own body. Those organisms aren't human cells, yet the human body depends on relations with them. That's a comparison drawn from what I know about biology, not something I saw happening beneath the walnut today. Still, it complicates the tidy distinction between an individual living being and a loose collection of parts.

The question of care remains just as ordinary: the swing is still hanging from the tree. What would actually benefit this walnut, rather than merely fit my calendar? I don't yet know. Recognizing that another life has its own history and possible good does not, by itself, explain how that life contributes to the intention I form toward it.

## Research Connection

I've been pushing back on [Raymond Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) while reading *Neofinalism*. He argues that a living form cannot be understood from a snapshot alone. In chapter 9, he describes an organism in terms of processes that unfold in space and time (trans. Alyosha Edlebi, 2016, p. 100). In chapter 14, he says living beings continue to form themselves rather than operate like finished machines (p. 147). These are philosophical arguments about organizing activity, not things I can confirm by looking at a damp patch of soil.

Ruyer also offers a stronger answer to my objection than I first wanted to give him. His account of organized wholes includes component individualities, sometimes relatively subordinated by the whole (pp. 143, 150–151). But what about relations among the walnut, soil organisms, and other independent lives that don't become its organs? Can there be genuine organization without a single subject surveying it all?

This is a question for my developing account of [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md), not a finding that settles it. The differing rhythms of our lives may matter to how I sustain an intention of care. But I still need to show what another being's *actual activity* contributes to that intention beyond shaping my beliefs, correcting my expectations, or determining whether care succeeds.

## Question Carried Forward

**How could another being's independently unfolding life help sustain an intention of care, beyond changing what I know or what successful care requires?**
