*September 27, 2026 · Black walnut · 25 minutes*

## Prompt

> Where is the unity of this tree?

## Field Notes

Looking upward, I could follow the walnut’s trunk into a fork, a limb into smaller branches, and then lose those lines among overlapping leaves. The branches moved in different directions and occupied separate patches of sky. Most leaves remained green, with a few yellow among them. Sunlight and movement entered through openings in the canopy.

What appeared first was not a collection of neighboring branches but one black walnut. Some continuity was visible. Memory and habit helped me find the tree again when a branch disappeared. Biological knowledge supplied other possibilities: shared circulation, development, and integrated growth. Calling this multiplicity one self-forming life added a stronger claim than the photograph alone could establish.

## Reflection

The trunk divides without becoming two trees. The branches receive different light and wind, carry different histories, and can suffer different losses. They are not interchangeable, yet their difference does not abolish the unity of the organism. **Organismic unity is not sameness among parts.**

Yesterday’s walnut–oak encounter posed the inverse problem. Two trees could occupy one visual field and materially condition one another without thereby forming one organism. Today’s branching canopy therefore sharpens a sequence: **co-givenness is not ecological interaction; ecological interaction is not organismic unity; organismic unity is not sameness among parts.**

That sequence gives [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) a scale discipline. A branch, an organism, an interspecies relation, a place, and a watershed are not larger versions of the same kind of whole. [Place and Agency](../concepts/Place%20and%20Agency.md) can name material and historical relations whose histories precede this sit and whose full extent exceeds present perception, while still preserving the difference between lives. [Vegetal Encounter](../concepts/Vegetal%20Encounter.md) keeps the question answerable to this tree rather than allowing the walnut to become an illustration of a ready-made theory. [Remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md) now includes the unclarified ground of a unity that experience gives more readily than it explains.

## Research Connection

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) explains much of the perceptual problem. In *Ideas II* §9, sensuous synthesis allows partial surfaces, hidden sides, and differentiated features to be given as one thing before explicit judgment. That helps explain why I can lose a branch among leaves while continuing to perceive one walnut. Husserl’s broader constitutive project also allows higher strata such as animate or living being, so the point is not that phenomenology stops at material objecthood. The unresolved transition is more precise: **what warrants moving from one perceived thing to one living organism?**

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) makes the organismic claim much more explicit in *Finite and Eternal Being*. She describes the living natural whole as internally cohering rather than merely assembled from outside. In the plant discussion, material is taken up and incorporated into the whole; she later says that every part belongs to the organism as a **member or organ** of one living unity. This gives a metaphysical answer to the dead-branch problem: physical attachment alone is not what makes something a living member. But membership does not by itself show that a particular local loss is *for the good of the whole*. That further judgment requires biological and contextual warrant. And the photograph does not prove Stein’s account in the first place. It makes the transition visible as a problem: what in experience warrants moving from one perceived thing to one internally articulated living whole?

[Vanessa Watts](../concepts/Vanessa%20Watts.md) prevents the distinction between organisms from hardening into a world of first-isolated substances. Her specifically Haudenosaunee and Anishnaabe account of Place-Thought challenges that starting architecture without requiring every relation to become organismic unity. In her governance discussion, she quotes Sharon Venne describing nonhuman beings as distinct nations that are at once sovereign, interdependent, interwoven, and related. That Indigenous legal and political context should not be reduced to a generic ecological metaphor, but it sharpens the pressure here: deep relation need not mean one ecological subject.

[Robin Wall Kimmerer](../concepts/Robin%20Wall%20Kimmerer.md) supplies a useful middle case in “The Council of Pecans,” a chapter woven through Citizen Potawatomi histories of removal, allotment, land, and collective life. Pecan trees can synchronize mast fruiting across groves; Kimmerer speaks of collective action, a “power of unity,” and mutual flourishing. Yet the grove remains a plurality of trees. Strong coordination does not require organismic fusion. Nor should the pecan case be universalized into a claim that all creaturely goods finally harmonize; here its force is more precise. **Collective coordination is not automatically organismic unity.**

The resulting guardrail is simple: **do not let ecology borrow the unity of the organism without argument.** One perceived thing, one living organism, and a coordinated ecological collective may each possess a kind of coherence, but the word *unity* cannot be assumed to name the same achievement at every scale.

## Question Carried Forward

**What in experience warrants the move from one perceived thing to one living whole, and, at larger ecological scales, what kind of coherence is actually present before organismic unity is invoked as an analogy?**
