*Friday, November 6, 2026 · Black walnut, Spartanburg, South Carolina · approximately 39 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> Can another being's independently unfolding good alter the very end of an intention, rather than only its informational basis, its means, or the norm by which we later judge it?

That was the question I came out with this morning. I didn't expect the walnut to answer it for me. I wanted to find out whether I could name one actual intention, distinguish what had changed in that intention, and let the philosophical account remain open to correction. Last night I had been reading about the fallen leaves and the lives they may shelter. That research had already changed a practical decision I was considering for the weekend. The sit was an opportunity to understand the change more carefully, not a way to pretend the tree caused an action I had already decided on.

## Field Notes

The weather surprised me immediately. I'd expected another cloudy morning after rain. Instead, the sun was out, with some clouds still moving through the sky. I came around the red maple, whose leaves are darkening toward red, and noticed the oaks shedding their own leaves and acorns. The walnut stood ahead of me with more and more of its upper branches exposed. The remaining lower canopy looked more yellow than green. Some of that difference comes from remembering yesterday's view, but the open branches and today's yellow are what I can see now.

I've been scrolling back through photographs of this tree from the last several months. They're extraordinary to look through in sequence, not because each photograph holds a complete life but because each gives one appearance through which I recognize the same developing tree. A particular picture shows the light, leaves, angle, and moment in which it was taken. It doesn't show the intervals I missed. The progression encourages me to think about the walnut's living continuity and about the history of my own attention, but I don't want to mistake either of those for a complete record of the other.

Then the squirrels arrived.

Four or five of them were moving near the walnut when I came out. They ran into the tree and began crossing its higher branches. As they moved, leaves came down. I followed one leaf as it detached and fell and managed to catch it in a photograph. That felt like one of those wonderful, accidental gifts of a morning outdoors. I could point to a particular falling leaf and say I watched part of its course. Most of the yellow sea lying underneath the walnut is different. I can see its arrangement, but not the individual histories of the leaves that now make it up.

The bark was still wet and dark, almost black in places, and sunlight sharpened the contrast between the trunk and the remaining yellow foliage. I could hear wrens, and I'd seen Carolina wrens in the yard. Off to one side, the oaks kept announcing themselves with falling leaves and acorns. During a later theological reflection, an acorn actually struck my shoulder. It wasn't especially gentle. The oak apparently wished to contribute a footnote.

The squirrels looked comically busy, but their enjoyment, intentions, and purposes are not mine to read straight off their motion. I watched where they went and what happened around them. Their lived experiences remain unavailable to me. The same limit applies more sharply to the walnut. The tree's branches are clearly part of what makes the squirrels' movements possible; that doesn't mean I can infer a shared intention or the tree's inner life.

Later in the sit I looked down again and noticed fresher yellow leaves resting above older brown material. I didn't rake, lift, or dig through them. The visible layering is worth describing. It is not a direct observation of the soil processes, eggs, larvae, fungi, or other organisms that may be present within it. Those questions take us into another mode of inquiry.

## The weekend action I was going to take

Here's the thing that made today particularly constructive for me.

Last weekend I ran the mower through fallen leaves with the mulching setting on. It worked as a way of tidying the yard. I had considered repeating that work this coming weekend, once the rain cleared. But over the last couple of evenings I'd been reading about leaf litter, overwintering insects, and the complicated history of claims about juglone from black walnut trees. What I found changed the weekend plan. I now intend to leave the fallen leaves whole in the naturalized area beneath the walnut instead of mowing them into small pieces or collecting them.

That's not a conclusion I reached by watching the squirrels today. The decision came out of the reading before I began recording. Clemson Extension and the Xerces Society offer good reasons to retain whole fallen leaves as habitat for invertebrates through winter, and to consider what those lives mean for the larger food web. Leaves participate in decomposition and soil processes. But I still don't know the actual species inhabiting this patch of my yard or what any particular individual will gain from the decision. I have a reasonable ecological rationale for a practice, not a local experiment completed with measurable results.

The juglone question is another good example of why the research matters. The older advice about growing beneath black walnuts often makes the chemical sound like an absolute prohibition. Some recent Extension guidance cautions that the evidence for widespread walnut-induced landscape damage is much less straightforward, especially where shade, moisture, competition, and site history could explain poor growth. Other Extension advice remains cautious around genuinely susceptible plants. I can't decide a particular groundcover plant is resistant merely because it is standing beneath the walnut this morning. I'd need to identify it, examine its condition, and make a fair comparison.

So I am not proposing a universal “never touch leaves” rule. In a naturalized area, whole leaves are valuable habitat. In a path, on a dense turf area, or where a particular plant is being smothered, there may be other needs. The question is whose good is served, and what evidence would allow a practice to be corrected. The planned comparison of groundcover beneath the walnut and two neighboring oaks still has to happen. Today I only saw the layers in one place.

A decision can be ecological without being infallible. It can be justified by credible evidence while remaining open to better local knowledge.

## Did my purpose change, or just my method?

This is where the philosophical question becomes much harder than it first sounds.

I already cared about the ecological life of this yard. Earlier in October I was talking about leaves as shelter for soil organisms, insects, birds, and other animals. It would be false to say that I woke up yesterday suddenly discovering the value of creatures I had never considered before. I also need to acknowledge that the repeated sits, the readings, and the broader concerns of my life have all contributed to how I think about this place. I cannot isolate one cause and make it responsible for every subsequent choice.

But something important might still have changed.

An overarching commitment isn't necessarily the purpose that governs each particular act. I might care about ecological life and still intend to mow a patch this weekend because I want it to look tidier. I can then learn something that makes other lives more salient and decide that the operative purpose of the weekend should be preserving habitat rather than producing a tidier appearance. That would be a change in what this proposed act, or purposeful restraint, is *for*. It wouldn't require inventing an entirely new ultimate commitment.

There is a serious rival description, though. Perhaps ecological care was governing the earlier intention all along, and the research showed that I had chosen a poor means. If that is the case, the end stayed constant while the intended technique changed. The outward reversal alone cannot settle which description is right.

I tried to separate four possibilities during the recording. I might gain information about the situation while keeping the same end. I might revise the means or the timing by which I pursue that end. The future condition of another organism might later reveal that my effort failed to benefit it. Or a newly decisive beneficiary might actually alter the immediate purpose governing my choice.

Those four possibilities aren't interchangeable. The third is especially important. My intention to do something good doesn't make the result good for those affected. Edith Stein's distinction between knowledge of a possible good and the events that realize it keeps that difference sharp.

At the end of the recording I say that I think what I'm trying to accomplish has changed, precisely because the decision to leave the leaves is now for the sake of the lives their cover may sustain. I want to preserve that first-person description, not explain it away before asking the question. But it remains a retrospective judgment about my earlier purpose. A stronger record would state the governing reason before a proposed action, then document the reasons, revisions, and eventual results.

That might sound almost comically careful for a weekend mowing decision. Yet it's the sort of ordinary action in which our larger philosophical claims ought to be able to survive scrutiny.

## Why Husserl doesn't let me off easily

My first comprehensive examination brought a valuable objection from Jack Bagby: what, exactly, does ecological intentionality explain beyond Husserl's layered account of intentionality?

I keep returning to that objection because it's right to demand an answer.

Husserl doesn't merely describe a motionless consciousness looking at objects. In *Ideas II*, experience, habitual familiarity, valuation, motivation, bodily action, and personal practical interests belong within intentional life. The world as experienced can draw my attention and motivate reconsideration, inquiry, and deliberate action. I can learn that an earlier expectation was wrong and redirect what I do.

A change of immediate purpose could fit that account very well. So the fact that ecological learning helped me revise Saturday's plan is not, by itself, a proof that I have discovered a different constitutive structure of consciousness.

Stein also raises the stakes for my argument. Her analysis of the phenomena of life makes room for qualified apprehension of plants without claiming access to an awake plant “I.” Her later account of finite goods asks me to distinguish what is good for another being from my knowledge and realization of that good. I want to preserve her discipline of difference, especially when it complicates a theory I am becoming attached to.

There's a meaningful countercase here, too. Last night the children were at LEGO time at the library. A handmade LEGO structure can matter deeply because of what a child was trying to do. An adult may change a plan in order to protect it, although plastic pieces have no organismic flourishing of their own. The meaning of the act can depend on human relationships and intentions rather than an object's biology. So changed purpose and genuine concern aren't exclusive to nonhuman life.

What the walnut adds, if something more is warranted, is a being whose continuing organization and development are not for me or dependent on what I happen to care about. It grows, sheds leaves, and responds to its conditions whether or not I observe it. Other living beings use this place without becoming participants in my personal purposes. Their independent activity and possible goods can correct my beliefs and my actions.

What remains to show is whether that independence helps *constitute* my particular conscious act or instead supplies the objects, information, motives, and later standards that Husserl and existing accounts of agency can already describe.

I'm increasingly convinced that there's something here worth constructing. Conviction isn't the same as having discharged the philosophical burden.

## A more difficult theological question

The Good Samaritan came back into the reflection. In Luke 10, the lawyer begins by asking who his neighbor is; by the end, Jesus asks which person became a neighbor through mercy toward the wounded man. That is a challenge to the way a question about obligation becomes a form of life.

I have been wondering whether something comparable could happen within Christian communities. A congregation might profess love of neighbor, or even care for God's creation, while very different purposes actually govern its budgets, land, buildings, and decisions. To change an operative purpose might be more consequential than finding a better tool for an existing program.

I know from my own religious upbringing how easily the natural world can become the background of a story told almost entirely in terms of human salvation. But that isn't the only Christian tradition, and I can't turn my experience into a history of every church or every theological reading of creation. The Hebrew Scriptures and New Testament, the history of Christian ecological thought, and the social and political conditions of any particular community deserve their own investigation.

The *Ecology of the Cross* needs more than an analogy about leaves. Philippians 2, Colossians 1, Romans 8, and 1 Corinthians 15 make distinct claims that must be read in their own contexts. A falling leaf isn't the death and resurrection of Christ. An ecological decision isn't automatically kenosis. And neither injury nor vulnerability should be justified by calling it redemptive.

The constructive possibility I want to examine is whether participation in Christ can transform what a community's purposes are actually for, including which human and more-than-human lives those purposes allow to question them. A possible class on Scripture and ecology might someday be a setting for that inquiry. It isn't an enacted or evaluated congregational practice yet.

This theological question and the philosophical question belong in conversation. They still owe their answers to different sorts of evidence and argument.

## After the recording: what the sources add

The subsequent source check makes today's claim harder, in a useful way. Roberta De Monticelli's chapter on rational agency discusses plans, commitments, policies, values, and competing reasons. Michael Barber's account of Alfred Schütz distinguishes a projected act from the various forms of doing and imagining that surround it. Both suggest that an existing phenomenology of practical life can already make sense of a broad commitment alongside a different purpose governing a particular action. I need to read their chapters more fully before claiming anything novel here.

There is even a historical caution about telling a story in which Husserl omitted everything that Stein supplied. James Jardine's research into the manuscripts of *Ideas II* suggests Stein's editorial work may have shaped parts of its discussion of motivation, while Husserl's own manuscripts already contain important accounts of it. The genealogy isn't neatly divided into the separate territories I'd sometimes like to assign to these thinkers.

For now, those are leads and pressure tests from a later source pass, not things I newly discovered by looking at the tree or a claim that the full works have been reread today.

## What I'm carrying forward

What began with the leaves and a proposed weekend chore is now a question about the order of practical commitments. I can mean to care for an ecology and still discover that another, more familiar purpose has been governing one of my actions. Ecological inquiry may rearrange that priority. It can also teach me a better means while the purpose stays continuous. And it may reveal that what I called care hasn't served the lives whose good I invoked.

I don't think the walnut has performed metaphysics for me this morning. It certainly didn't control the acorn that hit my shoulder. But the encounter, the earlier photographs, and the biological research have made the next test more particular and less comfortable.

I will have to record what actually happens this weekend rather than treating today's intended restraint as something already done. I'll also need to return to the living plants beneath this tree and the neighboring oaks, ideally with the same observational care I've tried to bring to the walnut itself.

The challenge now is not only to explain why a plan changed. It is to ask whether another being's independently unfolding good has changed what I am trying to accomplish, what might correct that purpose, and which part of that story a philosophy of consciousness still needs to explain.
