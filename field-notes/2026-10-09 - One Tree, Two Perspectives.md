*October 9, 2026 · Black walnut · sit duration recorded in private field note: 17 minutes*

## Prompt

> What changes when I look again from somewhere else?

## Field Notes

I took two photographs before sitting down. In the first, I looked upward through crossing branches and overlapping leaves. Yellow appeared among the green, with patches of sky between the limbs. I could recognize the tree without organizing the whole canopy at once.

For the second, I stood closer to the trunk. A low leafy branch came forward against the bark, with the yard beyond it. Individual leaflets were easier to distinguish. The branch was familiar, though its history remained a question.

## Reflection

Some things showed themselves by hiding other things. A nearer leaf interrupted the view of bark; one limb passed in front of another. The tree's unity did not depend on all its surfaces becoming visible together.

The closer view changed what stood out. It did not tell me how the low branch had developed. Looking from another position and learning a history are different achievements. [Remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md) needs that distinction: an obscured surface, an unwitnessed past, and an unanswered question about living processes do not all ask for the same kind of inquiry.

The photographs also have limits. They preserve framing and traces of where I stood. They do not preserve the whole encounter: moving eyes, changing sounds, or the possibility of turning and continuing to look. [Attention](../concepts/Attention.md) includes recognizing what a useful record leaves out.

## Research Connection

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) gives the changing perspectives a demanding first account. Unseen sides already belong to the horizon of what is perceived. Bodily movement opens further appearances; temporal synthesis holds the encounter together; habit and familiarity shape recognition. What appears can itself draw attention. None of this requires beginning with a spectator outside the world or inventing a new ecological structure.

[Maurice Merleau-Ponty](../concepts/Maurice%20Merleau-Ponty.md) raises a further question, but embodiment alone cannot be the answer. His early *Phenomenology of Perception* already develops bodily situation and transition synthesis. Its discussion of the camera and perceptual horizons also helps explain why a photograph cannot substitute for lived exploration.

In *The Visible and the Invisible*, the seer belongs to the visible, and the relation includes a noncoincidence that greater proximity does not simply abolish. Flesh does not make distinct bodies organs of one larger animal or establish that everything sensible experiences in the same way. The question I carry into that work is whether it changes how we understand perceiver and perceived as differentiated within a field they share.

These branches do not decide between the accounts. They make the burden more concrete. Even if flesh contributes something beyond Husserl and the early Merleau-Ponty, [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) still has to show what further work its own name performs. For now, the practice helps me distinguish a newly available view from a stronger claim about the being I am encountering.

There are now two questions to keep separate. First, does Merleau-Ponty's later account of flesh explain anything these embodied and changing perspectives cannot already receive from Husserl and Merleau-Ponty's earlier work? Second, even if it does, what would ecological intentionality add beyond that? The photographs don't answer either question, but they keep me from treating a new name as a discovery.

## Question Carried Forward

**What does another perspective let me see, and what still requires a different kind of evidence or argument?**
