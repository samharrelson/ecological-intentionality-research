*Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 17½ minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> How can I intend a future while remaining open to what the lives involved may require of it?

I came out with that question already in mind. Yesterday I had described a possible act of care and tested whether I could carry it out. Today I wanted to ask what remains open even after I can name an intention and believe myself capable of acting on it.

## Field Notes

I'm at the walnut later than usual. The sun is higher and brighter, with shadows in different places. Construction work and passing cars reach the yard. There are a few clouds, and the morning still feels chilly.

The lower canopy looks much thinner than I expected. I knew the leaves were going, but I had imagined another week or two with some yellow foliage remaining. There are many more leaves on the ground than on the tree now. The change feels faster than the future I had pictured.

Yesterday's canopy and gusts belong to memory. I connect the thinner foliage with that wind, but I did not watch the overnight sequence. The open branches are here now; their recent history requires more than this view.

The climbing dome remains where it was. I have not relocated it since yesterday's recording. I still intend to move it and have another position in mind, but I have not established that its present location harms the walnut or that the alternative would help. Shade, play and the tree's possible good all belong to the question.

## Reflection

### When I expect a change but not its pace

I want to be careful about how I describe being surprised. Yesterday I already suspected that the tree might lose its leaves quickly. I remembered gusts coming through, and I knew we were entering the part of November when the walnut might become nearly bare. I was anticipating the change. Yet I still wasn't ready for quite this much opening in the lower canopy.

So this isn't a story in which I confidently predicted one thing and the tree proved me wrong. My expectation was broad enough to include faster leaf loss, but the degree and timing of the change exceeded the picture I had been carrying. I can recognize that the canopy looks different today while admitting that I didn't watch the interval in which the change happened.

That seems worth describing in its own right. I don't encounter the walnut in a succession of isolated snapshots. Yesterday's canopy is retained in memory, today's appearance is in front of me, and I'm already wondering what the tree will look like in another few days. The present encounter has a past and a future within it. But neither my recollection nor my anticipation contains the walnut's actual course of development.

I'm interested in this because it gives me a modest example of being affected while I am consciously directed toward something. I came outside with questions about purpose and future possibility. A visible difference I hadn't fully anticipated interrupted those questions and made me consider the time of the tree's own ongoing life. That wasn't a new ethical decision or a completed change of intention. It was a particular way in which the world entered the present direction of my consciousness.

The canopy has unsettled a more specific expectation about timing. That is different from changing what I intend to do for the walnut.

I still have the same proposed move in view. What has become clearer is that carrying it out would not settle the future I hope it might serve. A new location could solve one problem and create another. Better evidence might favor leaving the structure alone. The walnut would continue living through either decision, with consequences I cannot declare good simply because I meant well.

An intention can be definite without containing a finished picture of its fulfillment. I can be directed toward a shared arrangement that supports play and takes the walnut's life seriously, while remaining uncertain about what that arrangement requires. The uncertainty gives me reasons to investigate, consider alternatives and return to the effects of what I do.

But I need to distinguish kinds of revision. Choosing a better location could change the means while preserving the same purpose. Learning that my idea of a good arrangement excludes important lives or needs could require reconsidering the purpose itself. Today's canopy changes my expectation about leaf loss. It does not by itself establish either practical revision.

### A future I can intend without possessing

The dome brings the problem into a more deliberate practical situation. Yesterday I tested whether I could move it and decided that I probably could. Today it is still in the same place. The capacity to do something has not settled what I should do or what the result would mean for the tree.

I placed the dome where it was useful for shade and play. Those goods didn't disappear when I began worrying about a nearby root. Now I'm considering a different arrangement, possibly farther into the yard, in which the children's play and the walnut's possible good can both matter. I haven't established that the present dome position injures a root or that another position will improve the tree's condition.

What is the intention here? It isn't exhausted by pushing a structure from one place to another. I'm intending a possible future of this shared yard. That future remains definite enough to guide inquiry and action, yet open enough to let what I discover about the actual place matter.

Suppose I learn that the dome is doing no harm, that moving it would disturb another area, or that there is a better way to protect a root while preserving play. Those findings might change the means. But there is also a deeper possibility: they might change what I mean by making a good place for my family and the walnut. I don't have to claim that such a deeper revision happened this morning to begin developing the philosophical account of how it could occur.

That distinction helps me say something positive about [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md). Another being's possible future may matter within the direction of an intention now. Its life need not wait until afterward to become a standard by which I judge success. What I understand myself to be doing can already depend on whom I seek to benefit and what I am prepared to reconsider for that being's sake.

This is also why [remainder](../concepts/Remainder.md) belongs within the relationship. Familiarity makes my concern more particular. It does not make the walnut's future mine to compose. Some unknowns can become clearer through inquiry; greater knowledge still leaves a continuing life beyond my project.

### Reception, directedness, and an unfinished good

I keep returning to a sentence from our recent work: the world is not only what consciousness is directed toward. Its independent lives and processes may participate in the very directedness of consciousness, even while remaining irreducibly other.

What does that mean in an ordinary encounter? At least it means I can discover that the purpose I brought with me isn't as self-contained as I assumed. I may have intended a convenient use of the yard, and then begun to intend a shared place in which another living being's possible good helps determine what I take a successful use to be. The possibility arises through repeated encounters, observation, practical experience, reading, memory, and further inquiry. I can't isolate one moment and call it the entire cause.

But I also don't want to define ecological intentionality as a history of learning. This morning's encounter matters **now**. I'm actively directed toward a future, with remembered experiences behind me and unfolding lives around me. Some of what matters to that direction has been given through a relationship I neither created nor fully control.

Perhaps receptivity is one way that an intention can be fully an intention. I can commit to a purpose without having every condition of its fulfillment settled in advance. Being open to correction doesn't mean that I am not directed. It can mean that what I'm directed toward is another life whose future won't coincide exactly with my plans.

And the remainder isn't just a blank space in my knowledge. The walnut remains a living being with its own ongoing possibilities even as I study its roots, leaves, bark, soil and neighbors more closely. I become involved with it, perhaps more intimate with it, without becoming the author of what it is.

I think that's what **intimacy with remainder** begins to name. The relationship can make my purposes more particular and more responsible while allowing the other to remain other.

### An actual future and a communal one

There's a related question I've been carrying through my years of church and classroom work. Groups of people don't always decide from scratch what they're for. Congregations form shared expectations through worship, memories, traditions, buildings, budgets, habits and teaching. Those expectations make some future possibilities seem obvious and leave others almost invisible.

When a congregation decides what its property should become, it can bring those familiar purposes to the land. But the water moving across the property, the trees and soil, the people who work there, the neighboring households and other living beings have their own histories and possibilities. What if sustained relationships with that place begin to change what the congregation understands itself to be directed toward?

That's not a claim that churches are one enormous individual consciousness, or that a congregation's plan has already been transformed by a watershed. I want to investigate how a genuine communal *we* can develop a shared direction through the activity and differences of its members, and how the nonhuman lives of a place might participate in specifying that direction without being members who vote or assent.

The question is not only how to make a preexisting church project greener. It is whether the relationship with a place might change what a congregation understands the project, or its common life, to be **for**.

## Research Connection

The philosophical proposal is that ecological relations can help specify and revise conscious ends. Today's case contributes a narrower distinction: corrected anticipation, a continuing practical intention, an action still ahead, and consequences still unknown. Whether the living relation also helps constitute the conscious act requires further argument.

[Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) gives part of this account an existing foundation. A selected primary passage in *Ideas II* §60(a) describes practical possibility through experienced capability. The earlier trial concerns what I think I can do. It does not settle what I intend the action for or whether that purpose will be adequately served. Ordinary motivated deliberation remains a serious explanation of the sequence.

### What the philosophers contribute to today's question

This encounter doesn't require me to begin by finding a fault in phenomenology. Husserl has serious resources for describing how anticipation is fulfilled or disappointed, how an embodied person comes to experience what they can do, and how practical life is motivated by a meaningful surrounding world. My changed image of the canopy and the still-uncompleted dome project can both be described through those resources.

Edith Stein's account of non-primordiality helps me take another's life seriously without treating it as something I experience from within. Merleau-Ponty's investigations of bodily involvement and noncoincidence open a different conversation about being intertwined with a world that remains other. Neither approach needs to be declared inadequate before I can ask what their resources make possible when the independent lives and possible goods of other beings become central to the formation of an intended end.

The additional question I'm constructing is how reception through ecological relationship enters what consciousness, individually and together, is *for*. I want that to become a positive account with its own explanations and consequences, not another list of things our predecessors left out.

The theological question has another starting point. [Mark 4:26–29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+4%3A26-29&version=NRSVUE) places sowing and harvesting alongside growth the sower does not fully understand. I read this kingdom parable as an opening toward responsible participation without ownership of the whole future.

For the *Ecology of the Cross*, I want to develop the claim that participation in Christ can form a congregation's purposes in relation to lives it cannot possess. [Colossians 1:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1%3A15-20&version=NRSVUE), considered after the sit, connects creation, church, resurrection and reconciliation through the cross. The ecological extension needs its own argument; neither passage supplies a grounds-management plan.

### Sowing, growth, and the life that continues

I was interested in [Mark 4:26–29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+4%3A26-29&version=NRSVUE) this morning because the person sowing is genuinely involved in an unfolding future without producing that future by an act of will. The person scatters seed, sleeps and rises, sees growth beyond their own understanding, and later harvests. The earth's activity is not an instruction to stop working. Human action and processes beyond human control both belong to the story.

This is a parable of the kingdom of God. I shouldn't turn it into a direct teaching on the physiology of walnut leaves or a ready-made model of environmental management. But it makes me want to ask whether Christian participation can include a way of acting that is neither withdrawal nor mastery. What if a community can intend, act, return, investigate and revise while understanding that it does not possess the whole future toward which it is directed?

That is a constructive theological question. The Ecology of the Cross will have to say more about Christ, creatureliness, participation, institutions and whose suffering is involved than this one parable can establish.

A congregation might begin by asking how to improve its property, then come to reconsider what kind of shared place it seeks to sustain. That possibility concerns a real communal “we,” formed through deliberation, disagreement and practice. It is a proposed inquiry, not a reported church outcome. It would require ecological knowledge, attention to the people doing the work, and a history of the place. Calling a project sacrificial would never make costs imposed on others redemptive.
