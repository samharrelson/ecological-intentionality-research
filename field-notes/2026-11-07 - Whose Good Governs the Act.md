*Saturday, November 7, 2026 · Black walnut, Spartanburg, South Carolina · approximately 54 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> When I say I want to care for this place, whose good is actually determining what I am trying to do?

The prepared question this morning concerned the black walnut and its neighbors, especially the plants growing at the base of the trunk. After yesterday's reflection about fallen leaves and a change in my weekend plans, I wanted to know whether care becomes more demanding when I can name some of the other lives in the picture. I also wanted to push harder on the question from my first comprehensive examination: does the living walnut change my intentions in a way Husserl's account cannot adequately explain?

That second question has been following me for months. It wasn't answered in the prepared script, and I didn't want to arrive with the answer already decided. But I wasn't pretending to be neutral, either. I increasingly think the fact that this is a living tree, rather than an impressively made copy of one, matters to the development of my conscious purposes. Today I tried to say why, even if my reasons still need a stronger philosophical defense.

## First noticing: the sky, the leaves and an ordinary Saturday

It's sunny this morning, mostly blue skies after several damp days. The air still has that chilly, wet-leaf feeling of November. I'm out here while the children are home for the weekend, looking at the walnut and thinking not just about philosophy but about what actually needs to happen in the yard.

I've been coming back to the same general view for weeks. Now the upper branches are remarkably exposed, with yellow leaves clinging lower down. The fallen walnut leaves make a broad carpet, but it isn't the bright uniform yellow I remember from yesterday. Some leaves are dark brown, almost black in patches, while newly fallen yellow leaves rest on top. I can describe those visible differences. What happened to each leaf overnight is another question.

On the way over I passed the red maple, whose leaves are covering another part of the yard, and one of the oaks, where acorns have accumulated thickly enough to make walking treacherous in spots. An old oak on another side of the yard has its own combination of leaves and acorns. One backyard, several different kinds of ground cover, several practical problems and possibilities.

A light breeze passes through and I see leaves coming down. Those falling leaves are happening now. The histories of the hundreds already lying beneath the tree are not. The photographs I've been making since September let me compare appearances, but they can't supply all the intervals between them. They also remind me that what I perceive now is full of memory, habit and expectation. That is not a flaw in perception. It's one of the reasons Husserl keeps finding his way into these mornings.

I'm increasingly aware that the tree's changing appearance isn't identical with my knowledge of the living processes producing it. Yellow, brown, bare branches and falling leaves appear to me. Biological development requires another kind of explanation, and the walnut's possible experience, if there is any, is not something I can read straight off those colors.

## Looking down: ivy, violets and lilyturf

Today I deliberately turned my attention from the upper canopy to the base of the trunk.

Yesterday I used PictureThis to begin identifying plants in this part of the yard. The app suggested English ivy (*Hedera helix*), common blue violet (*Viola sororia*) and lilyturf (*Liriope*). I didn't stop there. I spent time comparing photographs and sketches with plant books, field guides and other resources. That has made me more confident in the general identifications. Still, I need to write down the diagnostic characters and the exact sources that support them, and I haven't settled whether every clump of lilyturf is *L. muscari* rather than a look-alike.

That distinction sounds fussy until I consider what I'm trying to infer from these plants. Naming them helps me ask better questions. It doesn't tell me whether they're flourishing, what they're doing to the walnut, or how they affect insects, fungi and the other groundcover.

I can see ivy climbing the bark, perhaps about a foot up the trunk now. There are narrow, grass-like clumps and broader violet-like leaves among the fallen leaf litter. On another side of the walnut the violets appear more prominent. The arrangement is not uniform.

*At the walnut's base. A vine climbs the trunk among violet-like leaves, lilyturf-like foliage, and layers of fallen walnut leaves. The photograph supports the observation of placement and attachment, not a diagnosis of benefit or damage.*

Why are the plants distributed like that? I've wondered about light, moisture, the different sides of the trunk, and the history of how this yard was maintained. Those are hypotheses. I haven't measured the light or soil and shouldn't announce a cause on the basis of a photograph.

I also looked toward the two oaks. I recognized more violet-like plants around them, and a great deal of lilyturf near a stone ring around one oak. That isn't a matched sampling protocol. I don't have fixed plots, species counts, moisture readings or a reliable record of every planting decision in these places. But it does move the inquiry forward from “some plants near the walnut” to observations that can be revisited and tested.

There's something delightful about finally naming some of the neighbors of a tree I've spent so many hours thinking about. The names don't reveal their inner lives, but they do make my next ignorance more particular.

## One community doesn't mean one harmonious good

English ivy is a living plant. Its growth is not a moral failure because it happens to be a species introduced to this region. Nor does the fact that the violet is native give me immediate knowledge of its well-being or tell me what every management decision should be.

But it's also not true that every living thing in this yard has an equal claim to spread without limits. Clemson Extension identifies English ivy as invasive in South Carolina and explains how established growth can shade or smother other vegetation. That is general ecological guidance. It doesn't prove that this particular vine, visible for about a foot on the walnut's bark, has injured the tree. I want to know what's actually happening before I decide how to manage it.

This distinction reminded me of the bamboo along an adjoining property. I've had to work to keep its shoots from extending into parts of our yard. I sometimes jokingly think of that work as a negotiation rather than a battle, because I don't hate bamboo or deny that it's a living thing. But its vigor can create a very different situation for the plants and people around it. The same general lesson comes up with kudzu, another introduced plant familiar to anyone who has spent time looking at the landscapes of the Upstate.

It isn't enough to say “introduced, therefore bad,” or “native, therefore good.” Some introduced species are aggressively invasive under particular conditions, and some native species can also grow vigorously where we don't intend them. I was reminded during the source review that poison ivy and Virginia creeper are native to eastern North America. My personal annoyance with poison ivy doesn't change its biogeographic history.

The question is more difficult than sorting beings into two moral categories. What is each organism doing? What other lives and practices does its growth affect? Which ecological relationships are supported by evidence? Which ones am I merely imagining?

I love the idea of adding more native goldenrod, partly for pollinators and partly because I want the yard to support more of the relationships that already characterize this part of the Piedmont. But even that good intention requires species and site selection. Goldenrod in the wrong location isn't automatically an act of care, and I won't turn the project into an arbitrary requirement that every plant has to be native to count.

This is where [Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) keeps returning. Another being has a life that isn't mine. Its possible good can't be read off my sentiment toward it. And several living beings sharing a space doesn't mean they share the same requirements, much less a single experience.

## The purposes of mowing were more complicated than I first said

My decision about the fallen leaves has already changed this week's plans. I'd considered mowing through the walnut leaves again this weekend, and then I read more about leaf litter as overwintering habitat for insects and other invertebrates. I've decided, for now, to leave whole leaves in the naturalized areas rather than shred or collect them.

Yesterday I described that as a change from tidying the yard to caring for the lives supported by the litter. That's part of it. But today I realized that my earlier purposes weren't quite that tidy themselves.

I'd also mowed because I thought it would benefit the soil by breaking down the leaves. And I had reasons involving access, play and moving safely through the yard. Anyone who has walked across a dense patch of acorns knows that it can be slippery. Those are real practical goods, not necessarily expressions of an anti-ecological aesthetic.

So I have to reconstruct the before-and-after more fairly.

I had intended to mulch some of the leaves because I wanted the yard to look orderly, believed smaller pieces would be better for the soil, and wanted useful, safe places for our family. Now I intend to leave more leaves whole because I've learned about possible overwintering habitat and the importance of preserving what lives in the litter.

There is an ecological purpose in both accounts. There are also different beneficiaries, different expectations of what will happen, and possibly a new priority among ends that were already present.

Perhaps the right next action isn't “never move a single leaf.” Perhaps it is to leave substantial habitat where that makes sense, while keeping particular walking routes passable or managing an invasive plant where evidence warrants. I haven't enacted a complete plan for the whole yard yet. This morning doesn't establish that all the organisms here will benefit.

And I need to watch myself here. An intention's sincerity doesn't give me biological evidence of its success. Better information may lead me to choose differently, but knowing a possible good isn't identical with making it happen.

I suspect that point will matter as much to our eventual theological argument as it does to this particular Saturday.

## Husserl is still husselin'

Now to the harder part of today's recording.

I've been working since my first comprehensive examination with the challenge that [Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) may already have more than enough resources to describe the phenomenon I call ecological intentionality. It would be too easy to imagine that his phenomenology consists of a detached spectator staring at a passive object, and then announce that a living walnut proves him wrong.

That isn't his account.

The walnut is familiar to me through many experiences and anticipated possibilities. When I see its thinner canopy I see it within a history of earlier canopies, seasonal expectations and habits of returning. My body orients toward the tree, moves around it, and makes different aspects available. Its appearances can attract my interest and motivate investigation.

Within the personal and practical world of the yard, I value the tree, the soil, the family uses of the space and the possibility of other living beings flourishing here. I make plans, revise judgments and reconsider which purpose an act ought to serve. Husserl's *Ideas II* describes a world that motivates active inquiry and practical response; it doesn't require that I remain in front of the walnut as an unchanging observer.

Roberta De Monticelli's discussion of practical agency, and Michael Barber's presentation of Alfred Schütz, make the challenge even harder. A standing commitment to care can coexist with several immediate projects. Learning may change which reason governs the next action. The very change I care about might already be described within those accounts.

I think that is a strong objection. I also think there is something further to ask.

While looking at the ivy climbing the walnut, I began considering its own continuing growth. It has a biological organization that isn't given to me as one of my personal projects. The walnut's development isn't something I can stop and start with attention. These organisms will have conditions of life even when I leave this yard, and my decisions can meet or miss those conditions.

That's not the same as saying I perceive the ivy's own conscious goals. My description of its growth is partly informed by biological knowledge and partly interpreted through ideas of purpose and flourishing. I can't turn a visible climbing stem into a human-like first-person intention.

And yet I think the actual living organism has made a difference to my consciousness and my practical ends.

I don't mean only that the word “walnut” is a more interesting object of thought than “LEGO tree.” I mean that over these years the tree has grown, changed, surprised me, and made some of my previous plans seem inadequate. I've had to learn what it is, what its leaves and roots are doing, and what might count as care for its ongoing life.

When we first moved here, I even mistook its nuts for pecans. I was thinking from trees I knew growing up. The staining husks, the walnuts themselves, and some investigation eventually corrected that early identification. It wasn't just a prettier way to name a familiar object. Learning what kind of organism I was encountering changed what I could reasonably ask and do in relation to it.

I don't believe I'd have the same practical history with a LEGO tree in the yard. However sophisticated the model, the model itself isn't undergoing the same biological development, vulnerability or seasonal changes.

**That's the gap I keep trying to describe.** A living being has a history and an independently unfolding good that an artifact as such doesn't have.

But I'm not going to make the inference too quickly. A photograph of the walnut could motivate care for the *living tree shown in the photograph*. The image doesn't have to photosynthesize for the intended beneficiary to be alive. And a model could lead me to change my plans for real living trees somewhere else. So the comparison can't simply divide “living object makes me care” from “dead object leaves my intentions unchanged.”

Husserl can still describe my learned beliefs, memories, valuations and motivated practical responses to a real organism. The remaining challenge is to say why the organism's ongoing life is part of the **constitution of this particular conscious act**, and not only its actual object, the cause of my learning, the information motivating the act, or the independent standard by which later care might be judged.

During the recording I argued that the difference survives this Husserlian comparison. I still think there's something missing if we stop with the agent's reasons and representations. But I haven't completed the argument that establishes a novel constitutive structure. I want both things on the record: the positive claim I'm making and the serious possibility that Husserl can account for the example we've given so far.

## Stein, Merleau-Ponty, Ruyer and Watts don't give the same answer

There were several philosophical companions in this morning's digressions, and they aren't interchangeable.

[Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) helps me preserve the creature whose experience I cannot possess. Her account of vegetal phenomena of life admits a qualified apprehension without granting transparent access to a plant's wakeful “I.” Her account of finite goods also distinguishes what could benefit another being from an event that actually realizes such a good. She doesn't settle the biological condition of this walnut for me.

[Merleau-Ponty](../concepts/Maurice%20Merleau-Ponty.md) challenges the fantasy of an isolated consciousness surveying a world from outside it. His later account of flesh describes a shared sensible field, although shared visibility doesn't collapse the differences among individual beings or make their practical goods identical.

[Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) brings the organization of living form into sharper focus. The claim that an organism maintains a form of immanent activity or self-survey is a substantial metaphysical proposal. It isn't directly photographed when the ivy climbs the bark.

And [Vanessa Watts](../concepts/Vanessa%20Watts.md) creates a different and perhaps more difficult pressure on the entire exercise. Her account of Place-Thought doesn't begin by treating land as a passive setting for the choices of a perceiving human subject. But she works from particular Haudenosaunee and Anishnaabe histories, thought and political contexts. Her language of land and agency isn't mine to extract as an Indigenous endorsement of a new European phenomenological category.

I need each of these thinkers to be able to disagree with me, and with one another.

Maybe the developing account of ecological intentionality will have to say more about independently living beings, and less about the solitary human perceiver who decides which of them is worth noticing. That would be a substantive challenge to my starting point, not a bit of “relational” language added to a familiar picture.

## Beyond the backyard: what is a church actually for?

As often happens in these longer sits, I ended up thinking about Christian community.

In my own church experience, I've seen how easy it is for the purposes a congregation professes and the purposes that organize particular decisions to drift apart. We can talk about mission, love of neighbor and care for God's creation, and then make choices mainly around convenience, budgets, membership, facilities or institutional survival.

That isn't a claim that every congregation behaves this way, or that budgets and buildings don't matter. They absolutely do. But we have to ask whether those things are means serving the community's larger theological purpose or have become purposes that organize everything else.

Imagine examining a new playground, a building project or the management of a church's grounds. Who benefits? What happens to trees, soil, water and the other beings already there? Who is doing the maintenance work? Who gets safe access, and who is left bearing a cost? What would count as evidence that a practice is genuinely caring?

Those aren't abstract questions in our community.

The Good Samaritan in Luke 10 challenges how one becomes a neighbor through mercy, but its immediate subject is a wounded human being. Extending it to more-than-human lives requires a constructive argument. Philippians 2, Colossians 1, Romans 8 and 1 Corinthians 15 offer different Christological, communal, cosmic and resurrection claims, none of which is demonstrated by falling leaves or a more ecologically informed lawn-care decision.

I thought about the Lorax, too. I understand the appeal of someone who says he speaks for the trees. But perhaps the task of a church is not to replace the tree's own life with a human spokesperson. It could be to develop ways of attending, investigating, judging and acting in which the trees and soil are not merely invisible background.

This is a theological possibility I'm trying to construct, not a report of a congregation that has already achieved it. The proposed class on Scripture and ecology remains prospective. I'd want actual communal deliberation, history, appropriate consent and ecological evidence before drawing conclusions about institutional transformation.

And this connects to the walnut in an unexpected way. We can say we're caring for it, but the operative purposes of our next action, or our refusal to act, need to be answerable to the lives affected. I suspect a church faces a version of the same practical difficulty, though its theological warrants and institutional responsibilities add burdens of their own.

## What today's sit established, and what it didn't

I left the walnut still convinced that living beings matter differently to our practical purposes than models or objects whose value belongs to human uses alone. I don't think the distinction is merely decorative. This particular tree, along with its changing leaves and the plants growing around it, has reorganized how I notice and what I think I should do.

But I also have better reasons now not to tell an easy story about my own transformation.

The purpose of mowing wasn't only neatness; it included soil and access. The plants at the walnut's base are not one homogeneous community with one interest. My identification work is better than an app guess alone, but it still needs documented diagnostic evidence. I can see where ivy climbs while remaining unable to infer that it has injured the tree. And my recognition of the walnut's living independence is not, by itself, an argument that Husserl failed to describe my act of caring.

Today's positive proposal remains **a differentiated answerability of practical purpose to independently living beings**. Its philosophical burden is to show whether the other's ongoing life does something constitutive within a particular intention that the strongest existing accounts of motivation, valuation, embodiment and practical action cannot already explain.

If those accounts fully describe this case, I need to say that. If they don't, I need to identify the exact explanatory remainder rather than announce that trees are special.

That's where I think the next stage of the work lies.

## Question Carried Forward

**If an independently living being can become decisive among several already valued purposes, what would show that its ongoing life helps constitute my intention, rather than merely providing reasons, new information, an intended beneficiary or the independent measure of whether my care succeeds?**

*I still want to advance the positive claim. But I want an answer that Husserl, Stein, Ruyer, Watts and the walnut itself could genuinely challenge.*
