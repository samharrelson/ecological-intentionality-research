*November 5, 2026 · Black walnut · approximately 43 minutes recorded*

## Prompt

> What happens when a familiar tree exceeds my expectation? What would move me from explaining the change to knowing how to care?

## Field Notes

I come around the red maple by my usual route and turn toward the walnut. I thought I knew what I would see. The openness above and the yellow sea below catch me off guard.

Long upper branches stand against a cloudy sky. Yellow and some green remain lower in the canopy. The bark looks almost black after the rain. Fallen foliage covers much of the ground, where I remember a thinner scattering. That comparison brings earlier mornings into the present view without making them visible again.

*November 5. Exposed upper branches, remaining lower foliage, dark bark, and fallen yellow leaves. The photograph records an arrangement; it does not reconstruct the overnight events that produced it.*

During a breeze, I watch some leaves fall from their attachment points. This is a witnessed event, unlike the history of most of the foliage already on the ground. Rain, wind, and seasonal development belong among the explanations to investigate. Their possible involvement does not identify what brought down each piece.

A small long-legged creature on the bark draws my eye. I am unsure of its identification. Later I touch the wet trunk. Water clings to leaves and limbs, bird calls sound through the yard, and passing vehicles interrupt the quiet. My intended discussion of a particular bark feature gives way to the scene before me.

## Reflection

Familiarity brought expectations, but the walnut did not have to remain as I expected it. That is a modest proposition with consequences for the way I approach care. A purpose formed through earlier encounters must remain open to what another life is doing now.

The litter question becomes more insistent. What does leaving this material do for the walnut and its neighbors? I have not yet completed the plant identifications or the comparison of nearby groundcover. This morning renews an intention to investigate; it does not report a new management practice.

I need to keep two questions apart. What explains the fallen leaves? What should I do for the lives affected by them? Even a convincing explanation of the fall would not determine whether leaving, mulching, or moving litter is appropriate here. That requires knowledge of effects and a clearer account of whose good I intend to serve.

Care can direct inquiry before it directs an intervention. Inquiry, in turn, can require me to reconsider what I meant by care. It might also confirm the existing practice. I should not require a correction in order to call the investigation worthwhile.

## When the explanation is not yet the good

A small part of the morning gives this problem a shape I didn't anticipate. Water clings to some of the remaining foliage. A droplet on a leaf takes me back to things I know from teaching physical science: the way water behaves, the balance of forces at a surface, and the importance of water to living processes. But the particular droplet is not an observation of the tree taking it into its tissues. I see water on the outside of a leaf. Transpiration, uptake, and photosynthesis are different kinds of claims, requiring biological knowledge and, in some cases, measurements I haven't taken.

Likewise, I can watch a leaflet detach and move through the air without having seen the course of development that made it ready to fall. I can reasonably think about season, wind, rain, and abscission without knowing the full causal history of that particular leaf.

I am interested in the way the encounter brings these kinds of knowledge together without making them equivalent. What I see calls on what I remember and have learned. Those resources allow me to form questions I couldn't have asked in the same way earlier in the semester. But the relation moves in both directions: a changed scene can also show where my earlier explanation was too easy.

I intended to look more closely at a part of the trunk today. Instead I was drawn to the exposed branches, the growing yellow cover, a small creature on the bark, and the droplets left by the rain. The intention to observe wasn't abandoned, but it became more particular through what I found.

One difficulty is that this happened in a setting I have come to know well. The surprise is partly a consequence of the long practice of returning. The red maple, the turn, the place from which I see the whole walnut: these have become part of what I expect. I can't claim that the tree alone changed my consciousness. My practice, reading, memory, and prior concerns have helped form the expectation that the scene disappointed.

There is a further difference between the two inquiries that now confront me:

**What happened to make this ground so yellow?**

**What would justify changing how I care for this ground and the lives within it?**

A better explanation of the fallen foliage won't answer the second question by itself. For that I need to identify the organisms actually present, learn what available actions would do to them, distinguish the walnut's possible good from the possible goods of its neighbors, and explain why I should favor one practical end over another where interests differ. What I call care could be well-intended and still fail those lives. Equally, careful inquiry might confirm that I need not change the existing practice.

This is the positive possibility I want to keep working with: **the good of an intended beneficiary can make an inquiry necessary before an intervention is justified**. My intention becomes more specific when I ask what would count as evidence of benefit. But I have not shown that the living tree's independent activity constitutes the conscious act of asking. That remains a philosophical task rather than a conclusion drawn from the yellow ground.

## Research Connection

The developing proposal of [Ecological Intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) concerns how purposes become particular and revisable through relations with independently living beings. Today's contribution concerns the formation and specification of a purpose: attention shifts, an explanation is sought, and investigation becomes a more definite next act. Whether the tree's living activity constitutes an ongoing conscious act remains a further question.

[Charles S. Peirce](../concepts/Charles%20S.%20Peirce.md) makes the proposal both richer and harder. In selected passages of “Evolutionary Love” (1893), he describes love as encouraging independence while drawing its creations into harmony. He also follows the development of a philosophical idea into particular practical cases. These are useful demands on a theory of care: permit another's development, and let concrete situations make the idea more determinate.

Yet harmony cannot be my starting conclusion. The walnut, groundcover, and other lives may have different goods. I have not established a conflict here, but an inquiry that excludes conflict in advance would protect my theory from the very lives it claims to serve. This is a question I bring to Peirce's account, not a botanical claim supplied by his essay.

[Husserl's](../concepts/Husserl.md) account of a meaningful practical world and motivated inquiry remains a strong explanation of the conscious sequence. Surprise can also occur before an artificial scene; a made object can matter through its history. My task is to explain what the independent beneficiary contributes to this particular purpose, without treating surprise alone as evidence of a new structure of consciousness.

The separate *Ecology of the Cross* proposes that participation in Christ can transform communal purposes toward forms of communion that do not make vulnerable lives expendable. Its [theological statement](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/5) has distinct scriptural tasks. A congregation considering its grounds would need ecological knowledge, institutional history, and the accounts of affected people. Neither the beauty of yellow leaves nor a generous description of its mission could establish what its choices accomplish.

## Could a congregation learn to change its purposes?

This morning the question also extends beyond the yard. I have been thinking about what a possible church class on religion and ecology might become in the spring. A congregation could read familiar biblical texts alongside the actual ecological history of the land it occupies, the people affected by its decisions, and the living communities around it. That would be more demanding than adding a sermon about nature or deciding that every tree is a symbol of faith.

I am especially interested in the difference between a congregation changing a **method** and changing an **end**. Installing a new landscape feature, altering a purchasing decision, or earning a certification may be worthwhile. But what is the congregation trying to achieve in the first place? Whose accounts help define that purpose? What evidence could show that an action benefited the place and its neighbors? And could learning from affected lives require the community to change what it understands ministry to be *for*?

The Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) has an [Earth Care Congregations program](https://pcusa.org/about-pcusa/agencies-entities/interim-unified-agency/ministry-areas/hunger-program/sustainable-livingearth-care-concerns/earth-care-congregations), with commitments in worship, education, facilities, and outreach. I think those categories offer a real institutional place to begin. Certification would be a documentable achievement, but not a substitute for evidence that people, other organisms, or a particular watershed are better served. A class could help open the inquiry. It could not certify the adequacy of its own answers.

Luke 10:25–37 has become a useful theological challenge here. The legal expert asks Jesus to identify who counts as a neighbor. At the end of the parable, Jesus asks which traveler actually **became a neighbor** to the injured man. The Samaritan's mercy involves attention, changed travel plans, bodily help, money, and provision for care beyond the immediate encounter. It isn't only a kindly attitude.

The injured person in that story is human. I don't want to claim that Jesus was explicitly teaching an ecology of intentionality, or that the parable itself establishes the walnut as a phenomenal subject. The extension of Christian neighborliness toward other creatures is an **interpretive and theological proposal** that other Christian ecological thinkers have made before me, including John Hart in *Sacramental Commons*. It deserves careful argument, not a retrospective claim of novelty.

Still, the parable gives me a serious practical question: **Who becomes a neighbor when another life changes what we think our intentions require?** A church can call itself a good neighbor. A company or local institution can use the same language. But calling an activity neighborly doesn't establish its beneficiaries, its actual effects, or its justice. Public claims about benefits need documentary, ecological, and community evidence beyond a benevolent description.

This is where the Ecology of the Cross may have a constructive consequence distinct from the walnut practice. The Christological claims about communion, self-giving, the cross, and resurrection need their own scriptural and doctrinal arguments. They should not turn unavoidable suffering into something holy or demand that already vulnerable people absorb another institution's costs. If participation in Christ calls a community into a less possessive form of life, I want to ask how the community learns to recognize whose goods its choices serve, and whether that recognition can alter its purpose.

For now this is a **possible future teaching and research setting**, not an approved course, an implemented church project, or evidence that a congregation has changed. People and communities would have their own histories and consent to consider. Any later dissertation inquiry would have to be designed with them rather than making them illustrations of a theory.

The connection with the walnut is not that the congregation is one giant organism or that its purposes somehow resemble the tree's biology. The connection is methodological and potentially philosophical: care can become answerable to beings and histories whose realities are not exhausted by our initial intentions. Whether that answerability constitutes an intention or revises an intention already constituted remains the difficulty.

## Question Carried Forward

**What evidence would justify the next act of care, and what would show that I had revised its purpose rather than only its technique?**
