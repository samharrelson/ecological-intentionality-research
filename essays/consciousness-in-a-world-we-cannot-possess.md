*An affirmative statement of ecological intentionality | October 24, 2026*

This morning I stood beneath the black walnut in our backyard, looking at a swing loop hanging around a branch. The swing serves our children's play; the branch grew within the tree's own life. I could see a little space between loop and branch, but couldn't tell whether the attachment was harmless.

I voiced an intention to remove the swing for the cooler months, partly for the walnut's sake. That doesn't prove a theory. But it gives me an actual intention to examine, rather than another declaration that everything is connected.

## A positive proposition

**Ecological intentionality does not begin by asking how consciousness possesses the world, but how consciousness comes to be through relations with a world it can never possess.**

I propose that individual and shared conscious directedness develops within relations among differently organized beings, some with unfolding lives and goods of their own. They need not share our intentions or possess humanlike consciousness to matter to the formation of our purposes.

This differs from being merely *conscious of* an ecology. We can study watersheds and forests while imagining our purposes are independently complete. My stronger hypothesis is that even the capacity to form and sustain purposes emerges within relations we did not initiate.

I call this possibility **co-constitution without co-intention**. A tree and a person need not share one mind; a rock, fungus, artifact, and squirrel need not have equivalent capacities. Ecological intentionality must preserve their different forms of organization, not dissolve them into one conscious whole.

**My substantive wager is that another being's independent activity may help constitute an intention's orientation, without that being sharing our purpose or becoming something we fully know or control.** Otherness would then be more than a limit to knowledge. It could matter to the formation of directedness itself.

## What I still owe the argument

I might intend to remove the swing because I *believe* the living tree will benefit. Husserl's accounts of motivation, valuation, and changing horizons already help explain that. An identical-looking manufactured tree could elicit the same concern if I mistook it for a walnut. Correcting my belief could change the intention. That does not establish a new constitution of consciousness.

The tree's actual condition also determines whether removal helps it. But **what makes care succeed is not necessarily what constitutes the conscious intention to care**. We must distinguish ecological *influence*, *developmental formation*, and possible participation in the *ongoing realization* of intending. The strongest claim remains unproved. That is the argument I now need to make.

## More than phenomenology, without abandoning it

Husserl did not imagine a mind trapped inside itself, and phenomenology remains indispensable to me. Stein's non-primordiality guards the other's difference, though interpersonal empathy cannot simply be transferred to plants. Merleau-Ponty's flesh challenges the spectator's standpoint. Ruyer's organizing forms ask us to take activity seriously without mistaking visible bark for proof of inward experience.

Yet the problem extends beyond phenomenology. Bateson, Ingold, and ecological-enactive thinkers already propose ecological accounts of cognition. I do not claim to have discovered relational mind. I want to ask whether *distinct and potentially conflicting lives* contribute to directedness in ways not exhausted by an individual's mental content, a system's common goals, or affordances for human action.

Vanessa Watts's Place-Thought challenges a human monopoly on thought within particular Indigenous cosmologies. Melanie Harris's ecowomanism asks whose histories and vulnerabilities disappear when I say *we*. Neither is an ornament to a completed Western theory; both may require changes in my starting assumptions.

## The Ecology of the Cross: another constructive claim

My larger project, *The Ecology of the Cross*, advances a second claim: Christian creatureliness is not ownership of a world God gives us to command, but participation in lives we neither originate nor fully comprehend. In Philippians 2, Christ's self-giving challenges grasping after power. In Romans 8, creation groans in vulnerability and hope. The cross calls for solidarity without pretending suffering is good; resurrection resists treating injury and death as creation's final word. That argument needs its own Christological grounds. A damaged branch is not a crucifixion.

A congregation might begin asking not just how to use its land or water more responsibly, but **which lives and histories make our common life possible, whose goods are absent from our decisions, and what might need to change in our purposes themselves**. Ecological science, histories of land and inequality, and genuine deliberation would be necessary. A changed church decision wouldn't prove the ontology, but it could make the theology publicly answerable.

## An unfinished beginning

I don't assume the walnut has human intentions or that all beings share consciousness. I propose a problem for any theory that makes independent lives mere contents or surroundings of an already finished subject: **what, if anything, do those lives contribute to the actual formation or realization of directedness?**

If older theories already answer it, I must acknowledge that. The walnut isn't here to validate my philosophy. It has its own life, which is precisely why I keep returning.

**What becomes possible for consciousness, and for the communities it forms, when its purposes are allowed to remain unfinished in relation to lives it cannot possess?**

*First, the seeing. Then, the keeping. And now, the creating.*
