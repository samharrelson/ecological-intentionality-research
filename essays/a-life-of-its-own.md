*Stein, Ruyer, and Creaturely Difference*

*November 19, 2026 · Working essay · Version 1*

The vine climbing the walnut has become a useful question for my argument. On November 7, I described it rising about a foot along the bark. The vine and tree appeared together, but their attachment did not make them parts of one organism. Nor did that view tell me whether either benefited from the relationship.[^1] If ecological intentionality concerns how living relationships help form purposes, I need an account of the lives whose activities enter those purposes. I argue that their goods can become knowable while their lives remain independent of my access.

## Knowing another life

Stein's non-primordiality gives this inquiry a necessary distinction. In empathy, my present act is primordial, while the experience I apprehend belongs to another subject and is given non-primordially. The difference persists within the encounter. Understanding another's experience does not turn it into an experience I originally live through.[^2] This account does not establish that the walnut has experiences of the relevant kind. It also gives me no reason to conclude that its biological requirements must remain unknowable.

Stein's account of living form makes that second point constructive. In *Finite and Eternal Being*, a plant forms itself through a temporal development whose actual course depends on its surroundings. Each individual has its own life and measure; its relation to the species does not erase its individuality. This life is received within a larger created order, rather than self-sufficient.[^3] The walnut's dependence on its circumstances can therefore belong to an account that preserves its own organization.

Stein also allows a rational observer to know a good for a being whose striving is nonconscious. Such knowledge differs from bringing the good about: knowing what would restore health does not itself restore it.[^4] I can learn something about conditions that support the walnut without claiming its experience, then discover that my intended care failed to provide those conditions. Knowledge, intention and benefit have to remain distinguishable.

## What Ruyer adds

Ruyer asks how an organized being can possess unity without depending on an observer who assembles its parts. His starting comparison concerns the difference between a surface inspected from another spatial position and the unity of a visual sensation. Adding an internal spectator to explain the latter would require another explanation of that spectator's seeing. Absolute survey names a unity that does not need this additional point of observation.[^5]

He extends the account to living form through primary organic consciousness. Survey is active organization, encompassing processes through time. A living being's formation and maintenance cannot, in his account, be understood adequately as an arrangement inspected from outside. The form's own activity belongs to the explanation.[^5] This gives Ruyer a positive answer to the question of immanent unity, while committing him to more than an observation that organisms develop.

The difference from Stein becomes substantial here. She distinguishes the plant soul's bodily forming activity from the inner life associated with animal and human souls. Ruyer attributes primary consciousness to organic form, although this does not mean reflective human awareness.[^6] I cannot derive his stronger claim from Stein's account of non-primordial access. The two positions concern related questions, but their metaphysical commitments need comparison.

## Individuality within relation

The strongest objection to an easy synthesis is that Stein already supplies individuality, self-formation and natural measure. Ruyer is not filling an empty space in her account. His additional proposal concerns the character of unity itself: organized form has the immanent presence he calls primary consciousness. My field descriptions neither prove that proposal nor make it redundant. The philosophical task is to decide whether it explains something that Stein's account leaves unresolved.

Ruyer also complicates my wish to describe belonging without absorption. In chapter 14, he considers individual histories organized within a stronger individuality and argues that greater integration can relatively efface the individuality of components.[^7] This is an account of organization, not an ethical endorsement of domination. It nevertheless raises a question for my ecological proposal: when do relationships among living beings constitute an integrated individual, and when do they sustain relations among lives that remain independently organized?

The walnut, vine and observer cannot be assigned to one absolute domain because they interact. I need to distinguish an organism's own integration from the ecological relations through which it lives. Ruyer's account helps formulate that distinction, while his theory of component integration prevents me from treating every instance of belonging as the same relation.

## Different goods in one place

For a concrete comparison, I turn to Clemson Extension's account of English ivy, *Hedera helix*, spreading into a bed of coral bells, *Heuchera americana* ‘Dale's Strain’. Joey Williamson identifies both plants and describes the ivy beginning to compete with and shade the coral bells.[^8] Here, co-belonging names a material relationship within a shared place. It does not establish one organism or a common good.

I use “good” in a limited biological sense: what supports the maintenance and development of a particular living being. In Williamson's account, the ivy's continued expansion restricts conditions for the coral bells' growth. I interpret this as a divergence between the plants' goods in that situation. It does not make unlimited expansion the ivy's highest good or determine which plant I should favor. That judgment requires attention to the place, other lives and the consequences of intervention.

This documented interaction helps me return to the vine on my walnut. I have observed attachment there without establishing injury. The comparison gives me a reason to investigate the particular relationship and a way to distinguish the organisms concerned. Biological knowledge can sharpen that inquiry without supplying either plant with a human intention.

## What I can carry forward

I conclude that differentiated living goods belong in my account of ecological intentionality. The argument does not yet require Ruyer's identification of organic form with primary consciousness. Stein already supports a substantial account of independently developing life. Ruyer presses the further question of immanent unity and challenges me to specify what kind of integration my relational language implies.

This comparison gives my positive proposal a clearer commitment. A life can help specify what I intend while its conditions of maintenance and development remain a measure my purpose may fail to meet. Those conditions are open to investigation and can alter the beneficiaries or means of a project. I understand intimacy with remainder through this relationship: I can learn more, become involved and act, while the life concerned continues beyond what my purpose includes. Belonging becomes more demanding as I learn whose different goods it holds together.
