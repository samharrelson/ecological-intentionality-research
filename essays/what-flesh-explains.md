*Embodiment, Belonging, and Noncoincidence*

*November 17, 2026 · Working essay · Version 1*

I first drafted this section on November 16 and offer this revision for criticism.

I explored the black walnut’s bark with my palm, the back of my hand, and my forearm during a sit on October 20. I also shifted my weight and how I was standing. Those variations in contact and bodily position sharpened a substantial question I've been exploring. What does Merleau-Ponty’s late account of flesh explain about this relation? I argue that its contribution lies in articulating a sensible belonging within which perceiver and perceived differentiate. Noncoincidence becomes internal to that belonging. This is a substantive development of embodied intentionality, while the implications for ecological relation still require further argument.[^1]

Husserl already gives resources for describing the encounter on October 20. In *Ideas II*, bodily movement and changing appearances belong to ordered relationships: moving my head or changing my position opens further possibilities of seeing. His account also distinguishes the body as a material thing from the body as bearer of localized sensings. Touching bark involves both an encountered surface and sensation localized in my body. Physical contact, bodily sensing, and another being’s possible experience require different descriptions. My contact with the walnut can't, by itself, establish that the walnut experiences me. The body’s implication in perception is already a philosophical achievement before the late language of flesh arrives.[^2]

Merleau-Ponty’s early work makes this implication central to practical life. In *Phenomenology of Perception*, bodily spatiality is organized through actual and possible tasks. I can adjust my stance or reach toward a surface without first representing each movement and then executing it. The world already solicits bodily possibilities. His preface also refuses the ambition to possess the world through a complete account of its constitution. Opacity and worldly involvement belong to this earlier account. I can't give late flesh credit for restoring a body or a world that the preceding understanding of phenomenology had somehow forgotten.[^3]

The later work of Merleau-Ponty becomes more demanding when he describes flesh as an element and as the formative medium of subject and object. In the passages gathered in my research since May, the question shifts toward the sensible belonging that makes their differentiation possible. The seer is also visible; the touching body is itself tangible. Perception involves a being exposed to the field it perceives. Flesh gives this exposure an ontological articulation: the sensible world and the sensing body cannot be understood adequately as completed terms whose relation is subsequently added. Their differences take shape within their involvement.[^4]

Noncoincidence is also decisive in this configuration. In the touching/touched relation, the positions can pass into one another, yet their coincidence remains unfulfilled. I read this incompletion as a condition of relation. If encounter required complete coincidence, the distinction between encountering and encountered would disappear at the moment of success. Merleau-Ponty instead makes divergence, or *écart*, part of how access occurs. The thickness of flesh enables contact while preserving difference. This gives belonging a structure that neither reduces distance to ignorance nor makes intimacy depend on abolishing it.[^4]

Returning to the walnut daily for these tree sits helps me keep the argument disciplined. My October 20 movements were variations in external tactile exploration and bodily situation. They didn't enact the example of one hand touching the other while it touches something. Merleau-Ponty himself distinguishes these dimensions of touch, as well. Nor does the bark encounter show that my sensing and the tree’s possible sensing are reversible positions within a shared experience. A field episode such as this can test whether my concepts describe what occurred; it doesn't independently establish the ontology or supply the walnut with an interiority I haven't encountered.[^5]

The strongest objection to the argument comes from the earlier work of Merleau-Ponty. *Phenomenology of Perception* describes temporal moments as differentiating themselves through their passage, rather than standing beside one another as completed units. The present carries a genesis it cannot make fully present. Difference within continuity therefore precedes the later insights. The late text’s own acknowledgment of Husserl also challenges a narrative of rupture. Around the discussion of flesh as element, Merleau-Ponty draws on Husserl’s account of corrected appearances: an earlier appearance can be cancelled without being erased. Therefore, revisability, horizons, and the persistence of what has been surpassed don't establish late flesh’s novelty.[^6]

My response is to identify the late account’s ontological work through a distinction important to my field practice. More information can resolve a question about the walnut’s history; it cannot abolish the noncoincidence of sensing and sensed. The former concerns a gap in knowledge, while the latter concerns the structure through which knowledge is possible. Flesh articulates why access depends on involvement without becoming identity. I understand this as an extension and reorganization of earlier resources, giving their account of embodied openness a general ontological formulation. Its gain is to make the relation between belonging and divergence explicit, while leaving the necessity of a wholly new category unproved.

The resulting ontology also places limits on ecological enthusiasm. Merleau-Ponty rejects the image of a vast animal whose organs would be our individual bodies. His working note on world-flesh distinguishes the sensible world from flesh that senses itself. Shared flesh consequently does not establish a common organism or uniform sentience. For my inquiry, this is a constructive resource. Belonging need not absorb the walnut into my experience, or absorb both of us into a larger subject. Yet ontological differentiation alone does not identify the walnut’s biological organization, establish its good, or decide what care requires.[^7]

This is where the comparison opens onto ecological intentionality. The later dome and plant-ring sequence asks how an independently continuing life becomes consequential for what I intend: whose good enters a purpose, which lives remain outside deliberation, and what subsequent evidence can revise the undertaking. Flesh helps me articulate why such an encounter involves belonging without possession. A further account must explain how particular living relations specify the direction of a purpose, while distinguishing that formation from adequate care. The philosophical work lies in that specification; the fact of shared sensible exposure cannot perform it on its own.

My conclusion is a qualified affirmative one. Late flesh contributes an account of differentiated sensible belonging in which noncoincidence is constitutive. It brings the perceiver’s exposure and the enduring difference between sensing and sensed into one ontological account, extending questions already present in the earlier descriptions of embodiment and time. The walnut encounter does not demonstrate that this requires a new intentional category, and continuity with Husserl and early Merleau-Ponty remains substantial. What I can carry forward is a stronger formulation of intimacy with remainder: encounter can be formative while the other remains irreducible to my access. Ecological intentionality must now explain what the independent life of that other contributes to the purposes formed within the relation.
