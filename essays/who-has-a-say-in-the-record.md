# Who Has a Say in the Record

*Argumentative Genealogy and Reusable Revision in Ecological Inquiry*

*November 27, 2026 · Working essay · Version 1*

## An argument needs a history it can answer to

A philosophical argument can become tidier as its history becomes harder to recover. I learn which formulation I can defend, remove an overstatement, and explain the result through examples that now seem to lead toward it. The resulting account may be stronger. It may also conceal what the examples first suggested, which objection changed their significance, and why a once-promising inference failed.

The Black Walnut Field Journal has made this problem practical. I return to a particular tree in Spartanburg, South Carolina, record what I notice and think, bring the encounter into conversation with texts, and publish a selected account. My developing question concerns ecological intentionality: how relationships with independently living beings help form and organize conscious directedness. The fieldwork has repeatedly changed the claims I can make. The public form of the work should let a reader examine those changes.

I argue that a dated, selectively public record can make a failed inference available as a resource for further inquiry. The useful object is a connected account of the earlier claim, the grounds that challenged it, the decision to retain or revise it, and the questions that survive. Preserving these relations gives another reader identifiable points at which to disagree. It also makes distinctions available for use in a different case.

The cases below establish what this arrangement can make inspectable. Whether another researcher finds it useful requires an encounter with readers whose judgments I do not control. That distinction belongs within the method from the beginning.

## What I preserve when a claim changes

By an argument's genealogy, I mean the dated relationships among its claims, sources, objections, and revisions. A chronology of files can help recover that genealogy, but the reasoning has to be stated. Two versions may differ because I corrected a transcription error, consulted a source, remembered an omitted reason, or withdrew a general claim. Those are different events in an inquiry.

I therefore follow a claim through six questions. What did I actually assert? What evidence was available then? What challenge entered, and when? What did I retain, narrow, withdraw, or suspend? What remained undecided? Which parts of that sequence can another reader inspect? A claim that survives scrutiny also belongs in this account. Recording revision as the required outcome would make continuing with a well-supported judgment look like methodological failure.

The warrant needs to remain attached to each step. Seeing a branch, identifying its source, remembering what I previously believed, explaining a biological process, and judging what care requires make different demands. A photograph may support an account of visible arrangement while leaving the cause of that arrangement open. A passage may defeat my account of a philosopher without establishing my preferred alternative. A theological proposal requires its own textual and constructive argument alongside the field description.

Dates also do different work. The event may precede its recording; a source may exist long before I consult it; and a later interpretation may change what I understand an earlier record to show. I need to keep those differences visible. A saved earlier text can establish that I made a claim at a certain point. It cannot, by its date alone, establish why I made it or what caused me to change my mind.

This makes the unit of revision smaller than an entire theory and larger than an isolated sentence. I preserve an inference and the grounds on which it depends. In the walnut inquiry, a failed inference has sometimes narrowed the claim that ecological intentionality needs to explain. The larger question has continued through that narrowing.

## The mowing account and the reconstruction of a baseline

On November 6, I described a revised weekend plan. I had considered mowing through fallen leaves with the mulching setting and, after reading about leaf litter as habitat, intended to leave whole leaves in the naturalized area beneath the walnut. I asked whether another life's possible good had changed the operative end of this particular intention.[^mowing]

The earlier note already included serious qualifications. I acknowledged a pre-existing ecological concern and considered the rival explanation that the end had remained constant while I learned a better means. It would misrepresent that record to make it an entirely confident declaration of newfound care. Nevertheless, its account of the particular transition emphasized tidiness giving way to habitat.

On November 7, I restored purposes that this account had underdescribed. I had also associated mulching with soil care and had reasons concerning access and safe movement through the yard. The reconstructed baseline contained several goods. Ecological concern appeared on both sides of the proposed change.

This correction altered what the practical revision could establish. Changing a plan remained part of the record. A clean passage from an aesthetic end to an ecological end became harder to defend. The revised account could concern a different priority among existing purposes, a change of means, or a more definite understanding of beneficiaries. Deciding among those interpretations would require more than pointing to the outward reversal.

The negative result concerns the inference from changed conduct to the origin of a new purpose. The case taught me to examine the baseline before explaining a transformation. A retrospective description can give the earlier act just those features that make the later act look philosophically significant.

Another reader can compare the dated accounts and challenge my reconstruction. They can ask whether the later recollection is more adequate, whether several purposes governed the earlier plan, and whether the current distinctions describe the same act at the same scale. The published sequence supports criticism of the account I gave. It leaves the complete causal history of the decision and the biological effects of the proposed restraint open.

The reusable distinction is between a change in what I planned to do and a warranted explanation of what changed in its purpose. An investigator studying a different practice could preserve the intended act and its stated reasons before intervention, then compare the later explanation with that earlier record. My retrospective repair shows why such prospective evidence would matter.

## Return and the failure of a necessity claim

The November 12 account made a different kind of mistake. After moving a climbing dome, I had returned to the exposed ground and reconsidered the ring of plants beneath it. I proposed three moments of an ecological intention: aim, act, and return. I then assigned return an exclusive role in bringing an overlooked life into consideration.[^return]

The note also contained a tension worth preserving. I recognized that reception occurs before and during action. Yet I still claimed that nothing in the aim or act could have brought the ring into my purpose and that coming back was how lives moved among the positions around an intention. The stronger statement exceeded the qualification I had already made.

On November 13, I supplied two counterexamples. Someone could have asked about the plants during planning. Contact with them while moving the dome could have interrupted the act. Neither event was reported as having happened. Their role was to show that a later return was not necessary for an overlooked life to become relevant.

The revision retained the particular finding. Return had mattered in the reported sequence. It could be defended as one way a purpose remains answerable through time. The claim that it supplied the exclusive route needed to be withdrawn.

An associated correction concerned the positions I had assigned to lives around a purpose. Intended beneficiaries, affected lives, and lives omitted from deliberation can overlap. The ring could have been affected by the arrangement while remaining absent from my planning. The three categories became questions to ask about an undertaking, with each question capable of receiving its own answer.

This history is useful because its challenge has a different warrant from the mowing correction. A later recollection complicated the mowing baseline. Here hypothetical possibilities defeated a general necessity claim. Treating every correction as fresh empirical evidence would obscure what did the argumentative work.

A reader can test whether the alternatives are coherent and whether they address the actual claim. They can also challenge my use of “return.” If I expand that term to include every possible reception before, during, and after an act, I may protect its universality by making it explain too little. Preserving the narrower formulation gives that criticism a definite target.

The transferable result is a distinction between the route by which a change occurred in a case and the route by which it had to occur. The case supports the first while the counterexamples challenge the second. Another inquiry can use that distinction without accepting ecological intentionality as its framework.

## A record can enter the history it helps reconstruct

The November 26 reflection introduced a further problem. A November 11, 2025 walnut entry had carried a Willie Nelson association. The song returned in the November 25, 2026 reflection. After that sit and its publication, the older entry was recovered; on November 26, I used the comparison to examine the practice's history.[^retrieval]

The earlier entry establishes recurrence. Its later recovery gives me something available for present interpretation. It also makes a new causal hypothesis tempting: perhaps the earlier encounter helped form the later attention. That possibility requires evidence beyond the recurrence and the date of retrieval.

Here the archive introduces a hypothesis whose evidential limits I examine before adopting it. A history of failures should preserve the difference between a possibility considered and a claim advanced. Otherwise, I could turn every possibility I decline to affirm into another apparent instance of rigorous self-correction.

The record is also active within the inquiry. Recovering it changes what I can compare now. The archive can therefore be both evidence about an earlier occasion and a present condition of reflection. Dating its uptake prevents me from projecting my current access backward.

The branch sequence adds a documented correction and its reported later use. On October 28, I removed a piece lodged in the walnut and publicly left the source of the break unidentified. On November 13, I reported an earlier association with the walnut and corrected it through comparison with a broken oak limb. The walnut's loss of foliage had opened a view that was previously obstructed. A prompt to look toward other trees, my movement across the yard, and the comparison of break, texture, and color all entered the sequence.[^branch]

The October 28 public uncertainty remains accurate as a record of the earlier inquiry. The correction concerns an association reconstructed on November 13 and the new attribution supported then. Keeping that distinction prevents the methods essay itself from inventing a more decisive earlier error.

On November 27, I described considering walnut and oak when approaching four newly fallen branches. I identified them as oak through comparison and credited the earlier correction with helping me recognize them. Later in the reflection I described the identification as quicker. Those reports supply evidence of how I understand my learning, alongside the present grounds of identification.[^nov27]

The three questions remain separable: what supported the earlier correction, what supports the new identification, and what supports the proposed developmental connection between them. The photographs and comparisons do not isolate the earlier correction as the cause of recognition. My report of increased speed is also unmeasured. What can be examined is the discrimination now available, the comparisons I seek, and the history I give of acquiring them.

This is a constructive result for the method. A correction can have an afterlife within inquiry. To trace that afterlife, I need evidence about its later use in addition to evidence that the original correction was warranted.

## What the method inherits

Environmental phenomenology already makes situated experience a source for inquiry and requires more than a researcher's confidence in the account. David Seamon describes firsthand environmental investigation and discusses reliability through interpretive appropriateness, public scrutiny, and intersubjective corroboration. His criticism of accounts that overlook the meanings places have for inhabitants also challenges the limits of an outsider's description.[^seamon]

My repeated looking, movement, recording, and return belong within that inheritance. The dated corrections give a reader a way to follow how a description bears on an interpretation across occasions. They extend a demand for scrutiny that is already present in the approach. First-person description also leaves questions whose answers require botanical evidence, historical inquiry, or other people's accounts.

Tony Adams, Stacy Holman Jones, and Carolyn Ellis describe autoethnography through personal experience examined in relation to cultural practices and relationships. Reflexivity includes the researcher's position and participation, while process consent continues through design, fieldwork, writing, and sharing.[^autoethnography] I use this defined approach as a comparator within the broader ethnographic family.

It presses the question of why the mowing story became compelling to me. My commitments, access to a yard, available time, and desire to write a constructive philosophical account help organize the research. Reflexive attention can examine those conditions while allowing other people to retain accounts and purposes that differ from mine. Acknowledging my position does not exhaust the work of understanding it.

Jean-Claude Bradley's account of open notebook science asks for access to raw experimental records, including failed and aborted experiments. He wants a reader to see what was and was not done behind a condensed report.[^bradley] That provides a demanding comparison with my curated public record, whose recordings and preparatory materials remain private.

I take from Bradley the value of keeping an unsuccessful attempt connected to the evidence needed to assess it. Here the attempt may be an inference, and reuse may consist in criticizing or adapting a distinction. Experimental repetition, argumentative scrutiny, and the recovery of a dated text remain different achievements.

These approaches bring complementary questions to the same cases. Phenomenological description asks whether the account fits the encounter. Reflexive inquiry examines the situated relationships and commitments entering its interpretation. A public record of revision lets a reader compare the claim with its later disposition. My contribution is the worked coordination of those tasks around identifiable inferences, including the different kinds of evidence that can change them.

## Who controls the public history

The public record is selected by the person whose argument is being tested. That is a constitutive limit of this arrangement. I choose which cases to narrate, which sources to foreground, and which failures can responsibly be disclosed. Published corrections are therefore a selected set, with no claim to represent every difficulty in the research.[^practice]

The technical publication boundary can keep designated private files out of an export. Editorial judgment still determines what a paragraph reveals, whose account it conveys, and whether a relationship is recognizable. A successful transfer of files establishes none of those judgments on its own.

Other people can participate in an activity without agreeing to its public representation. Their agreement about an action cannot stand as evidence of permission to recount their reasons. The absence of a publication discussion in a note likewise does not establish that no such discussion occurred. Adams and colleagues' account of continuing consent gives these questions a relational frame; the particular facts still need to be established.

Versioning creates a further difficulty. An earlier interpretation may remain retrievable after the current page changes. That helps preserve an argument's history, while personal material can require removal from the versions under my control. The method must allow such correction and explain any resulting evidential gap without disclosing the material again. A complete archive is not a condition I can demand at another person's expense.

The authority to identify a correction also needs examination. Linda Tuhiwai Smith asks who defines a research problem, whose purposes it serves, who gains from its outcomes, and to whom the researcher is accountable. Those questions challenge the authority I exercise before a conclusion becomes public.[^smith] Melanie Harris's account of ecomemory locates environmental knowledge within Black women's situated histories and experiences, including the agency involved in telling them.[^harris] These are distinct methodological challenges, whose contexts matter.

Their significance here is that the availability of a record cannot make my criteria of adequacy universal. People with relevant experience may contest the question, the account of harm, or the proposed benefit before they contest my conclusion. Published Indigenous thought and ecowomanist method can challenge the terms of this project. Citing them does not turn the walnut practice into either of those methodologies.

The walnut can constrain my account through changes that resist it. That constraint differs from a person's authority to challenge their representation or withhold permission, and from a reader's assessment of an inference.

Publicity can also shape the inquiry. A daily series encourages a satisfying development; a roadmap makes progress countable; a correction can display the researcher's carefulness. I cannot determine the effect of those incentives by announcing that I recognize them. I can keep unchanged conclusions, suspended judgments, and unresolved objections visible, and ask readers whether my selected history has made every outcome look favorable.

Preparation belongs in that account. I use AI tools in drafting prompts, reviewing sources, and preparing editorial revisions. I remain responsible for what I record, verify, revise, and publish. The public notes distinguish what the prompt supplied from what the recording added. Repeating a prepared proposition beside a tree supplies a record of saying it there; it does not independently establish that the encounter generated it.

## What another reader can do with a correction

The four histories make a common procedure concrete. Each reconstruction should supply an earlier claim or uncertainty, the grounds that bear on it, a dated decision, the remaining question, and an accessible record. The claim's scope and the kind of warrant must travel with it.

| Reader's question | What the return case makes available |
| --- | --- |
| What was asserted? | A later return was assigned an exclusive role in bringing omitted lives into consideration. |
| What challenged it? | Possible interventions during planning and action show alternative routes. |
| What changed? | The claim narrows from necessity to a route that mattered in the reported case. |
| What remains? | The prevalence and distinctive explanatory role of return remain open. |
| What could I reuse? | Distinguish how a change occurred from how it must occur, then test the latter claim with relevant alternatives. |

This procedure can be carried in ordinary notes, an article's appendix, or a public archive. The tools matter insofar as a reader can retrieve the relevant versions and understand their relationship. A repository's commit history helps preserve and locate text. It cannot decide whether a counterexample works or whether the narrower conclusion follows.

The source versions cited here provide one completed form of access. A reader can compare the November 12 formulation with the November 13 repair and judge the inference without adopting my entire philosophical project. They can use the distinction in another account of learning or action. That is an available task; actual use remains something to document when it occurs.

A test of the method should begin with a reader who has not helped prepare the reconstruction. I would ask that reader to recover the earlier claim, identify the grounds for its revision, state the surviving conclusion, and describe one disagreement the public evidence permits. The reader could then try the distinction in another case and report what failed to carry across. Disagreement would be informative even if the reader rejects my conclusion.

This proposal also states conditions under which the method's claimed usefulness would weaken. A reader may find that the earlier claim has been paraphrased unfairly, that essential grounds remain unavailable, or that the supposed distinction collapses when applied elsewhere. A comparison with the current polished account might show that the version history adds little. The work of preserving and navigating versions may also cost more than the clarification it provides.

Such outcomes would require revisions to the method itself. I cannot count published pages, completed checks, or a reader's willingness to open a link as evidence that the record has improved scholarship. The method's contribution is assessed through what the record enables a reader to understand, challenge, and undertake.

## What the failed inferences contribute

The mowing correction makes the earlier practical baseline harder to simplify. The return correction separates a useful sequence from a necessary structure. The recovered entry distinguishes recurrence from a causal history. The branch sequence separates the grounds of a corrected judgment from the evidence for its later influence. Together, these results make the next argument more demanding and more definite.

They also help me construct ecological intentionality affirmatively. I can investigate how living developments alter access to evidence, how encounters form capacities for discrimination, how an inquiry acquires its particular object and direction, and how its success remains answerable to a continuing life. Husserlian accounts of affection, habit, and motivated inquiry remain serious resources for explaining those connections.[^husserl] The methods account preserves where further philosophical work is required.

The same discipline applies to the Ecology of the Cross. A proposal about participation in Christ forming communal purposes needs theological argument and evidence about any community it concerns. A dated field record can show how I came to ask that question. Its chronology cannot supply the theological warrant.

A correction becomes valuable when its reason remains available beyond the moment of correction. I want a reader to be able to find the point at which an attractive story failed, assess what I preserved from it, and ask a better question of another situation. The record earns its place in the research through that possibility of further inquiry, including inquiry that changes my account again.
