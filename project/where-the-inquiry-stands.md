*Updated November 1, 2026 · Black Walnut Field Notes · An argument in progress*

For the past several weeks, I've been returning almost daily to a black walnut tree in Spartanburg, South Carolina. What began as an effort to develop an account of [ecological intentionality](../concepts/Ecological%20Intentionality.md) has become a more demanding inquiry into perception, embodiment, living form, and what responsible ecological attention might require.

The encounters have been wonderfully ordinary. I've watched leaves turn, followed branches through the canopy, touched familiar places on the bark, noticed fallen wood, and occasionally been interrupted by a squirrel. Alongside those encounters, I've been reading phenomenology, ecological thought, and theology. The texts help me notice more carefully, but they also make some of my earlier claims harder to defend.

I'm less certain than I was at the end of my first comprehensive examination that ecological intentionality names a new structure of consciousness. That isn't a problem to work around. It has become one of the central questions of the research.

**I want to find out what the encounters and the texts actually warrant, even if that requires changing or relinquishing a concept I began with.**

**New constructive essay (October 24):** Consciousness in a World We Cannot Possess puts forward my affirmative proposal for ecological intentionality, including what I think it contributes and the philosophical work still needed to defend it. The field notes remain where that proposition is tested.

## November 1: from an intention to an enacted and revisable purpose

This morning's [After the Swing](../field-notes/2026-11-01%20-%20After%20the%20Swing.md) begins November with an enacted action rather than a contemplated one. I took the swing down on October 31, mainly out of concern for the walnut. Today I saw indented, smoother bark at places I remember the straps hanging. I cannot infer damage to living tissue or improvement in the tree's health from those marks, and they were noticed *after* the removal, not used as evidence before my decision.

A remembered spider's web complicates the account in a productive way. Earlier in October I delayed removing the swing to avoid disturbing a spider and its web. When I later found the web absent, I decided the time had come. I don't know what became of the spider. What I can describe is **care becoming specific through several independently living others**, not a single good that made the correct action obvious from the start.

**My affirmative philosophical proposal for November is the ecological specification and revisability of intentional ends:** a familiar human purpose can acquire or change its practical content through encounters with other lives that neither share nor exist for that purpose. I want to explain the process by which concerns become decisions, including waiting, doing, and remaining open to correction. I must still distinguish a transformed end from a more specific end or new means, and develop how this relates to the *realization* of intentional activity. Rival theories of agency and ecological learning will test a positive explanation rather than indefinitely defer it.

All Saints' Day also sharpens my distinct theological proposal: in the *Ecology of the Cross*, I am arguing that communion in Christ may require a community to reconsider **what** it seeks in the presence of neighbors and living places it cannot possess. That claim needs its own Christian textual and ecclesial warrants, not confirmation by a tree's bark. The dated [public theological working thesis](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/5) remains an opening argument, not a completed proof.

## The October 24 turn: From description to a proposition

After the October 21 outline and two months of close comparison with existing philosophers, I'm making a deliberate constructive turn. I don't want ecological intentionality to mean only a method for noticing the natural world or an ethic of paying better attention. Those remain valuable practices, but the concept is meant to pose a more fundamental question about how consciousness and shared purposes come to be.

**Ecological intentionality does not begin by asking how consciousness possesses the world, but how consciousness comes to be through relations with a world it can never possess.**

That isn't an accusation that Husserl believed consciousness owned or manufactured the world. His analyses of passivity, embodiment, temporal horizons, and motivated inquiry remain among the strongest explanations of what happens in these encounters. It's a positive philosophical proposal: conscious directedness may take form through relations with other beings whose lives unfold without sharing our intentions. **Co-constitution without co-intention** is a working formulation, not a demonstrated new mental faculty or a claim that the whole world shares one consciousness.

The distinction matters because knowing about ecological dependence is not automatically the same as consciousness *being constituted* through ecological relations. Gregory Bateson, Tim Ingold, ecological-enactive thinkers, and others have already made important arguments for ecological cognition. My task is not to announce their work as my discovery. It is to ask whether relationships among differently organized and sometimes conflicting lives matter to the organization of intentional activity in ways that require a further account. I'm reading and testing those predecessors before claiming an answer.

[The October 24 walnut encounter](../field-notes/2026-10-24%20-%20A%20Purpose%20the%20Tree%20Does%20Not%20Share.md) makes the problem concrete. I saw a small gap between the swing's hanging loop and a branch, then voiced an intention to remove the swing partly for the tree's good. Whether the tree actually benefits depends on its condition, which I don't yet know. That's different from whether the tree's independently unfolding life helps **constitute the conscious intention itself**. A familiar Husserlian account of valuation and motivated action may explain the change of purpose; [Ruyer's](../concepts/Ruyer.md) account of immanent organizing form offers another powerful rival. Neither the gap nor my concern settles the stronger thesis.

This turn also matters for *The Ecology of the Cross*. A church or spiritual community may ask whether the lives and histories sustaining it should change **what it intends**, not merely how it achieves familiar aims. But ecological interdependence cannot establish Christology on its own, and a changed institutional purpose doesn't prove a new theory of consciousness. Christian claims about incarnation, the cross, resurrection, creaturely vulnerability, and participation require their own argument. [Vanessa Watts](../concepts/Vanessa%20Watts.md) challenges an exclusively human origin of thought from within particular Indigenous cosmologies; [Melanie Harris](../concepts/Melanie%20Harris.md) makes historical power, eco-memory and environmental justice indispensable to any public “we.” I want those conversations to challenge this constructive work, not merely adorn it.

**The immediate test:** Can I identify what the other being's ongoing activity contributes to the *realization* of a conscious intention beyond influencing my beliefs, motivating concern, providing an affordance, or determining whether my action succeeds? The October 24 sit gives me a real intention to analyze, not yet a decisive answer.

**October 25 continuation:** In [The Branch and the Purpose](../field-notes/2026-10-25%20-%20The%20Branch%20and%20the%20Purpose.md), the swing was still attached. The family's use of it came back to me through memory, and the visible branch brought renewed questions about care. Ruyer's *Neofinalism* (ch. 5, pp. 36–37, 44) already describes human purposes extending into organized tools without turning those tools into organs. That makes the next constructive question harder and better: **what changes when an activity we organize for human purposes also depends upon another living being with a development and possible good that do not belong to our activity?** The practical and biological standards may differ. Whether their difference is *constitutive of consciousness* remains unresolved.

**October 26 continuation:** [Moving into Shade](../field-notes/2026-10-26%20-%20Moving%20into%20Shade.md) adds an unplanned bodily experience to the prepared questions. As the sun bothered my eyes, I found myself standing behind the trunk to keep looking comfortably, without first issuing myself a verbal instruction. That is an immediate example of bodily directedness, which early Merleau-Ponty's motor intentionality already helps explain. A screen could have relieved the glare too. The walnut's *living organization* hasn't yet been shown to constitute this particular act. The philosophical advance is to specify **which act we're explaining** rather than move evidence silently from looking comfortably to caring for the tree. Ruyer's discussion of organized wholes and partly subordinated component individualities (*Neofinalism*, ch. 14, pp. 143, 150–151) also makes another question newly important: what would it mean for distinct living activities to contribute to one another **without being absorbed into one whole**? That's a constructive problem, not an established discovery.

**October 27 continuation:** In [More Than One Time](../field-notes/2026-10-27%20-%20More%20Than%20One%20Time.md), I came back to the walnut with Ruyer's account of forms unfolding in space and time. The observed morning was ordinary: the forecast fog had lifted, the soil and leaves were damp, and the light grew brighter as I stood there. The less ordinary part was where the reading took my thinking. I began with soil and its invisible life and found myself comparing it with bacteria in and on a human body. Ruyer already has a powerful account of higher wholes organizing component individualities, so pointing to many organisms together doesn't refute him. But it leaves a question I want to keep open: **Can distinct lives participate in an organized relation without being absorbed into one subject or purpose?** And even if they can, what would show that such a relation helps *constitute an intention*, rather than supply biological support, information, or a standard for successful care? Ruyer's *Neofinalism* (trans. Edlebi, 2016, ch. 9 p. 100; ch. 14 pp. 143, 149–153) offers a demanding comparator, not an answer supplied by the sit. My constructive theology of resurrection and creaturely difference requires its own textual case.

**October 28 continuation:** [What the Branch Leaves Open](../field-notes/2026-10-28%20-%20What%20the%20Branch%20Leaves%20Open.md) brings an action into the inquiry. Before the sit, I found a branch hanging in the walnut, thought it was damaged, and pulled it down. Then, while looking at the tree that remained, I noticed another feature high on the trunk and wondered whether months of returning here were changing what catches my attention. That gives me two things to keep apart: what my familiarity led me to notice, and whether I acted in a way that benefited the walnut. I've started separating **why I acted**, **whether the tree benefited**, and **what actually made the intention possible**. Those aren't three names for one question. Husserl may already explain the shift of attention, and Ruyer helps ask about living membership without letting me diagnose a branch from a photograph. I still need an account of how this particular living being's activity would help *constitute* my conscious intention, not only give me something to notice or a reason to reconsider my care.

**October 29 continuation:** [What the Leaves Change](../field-notes/2026-10-29%20-%20What%20the%20Leaves%20Change.md) gives me a new, differentiated set of events. An unexpectedly yellow canopy stopped me before I meant to sit. During the recording, three falling leaves drew my eyes away from a cluster I had intended to examine. Later, I wanted to touch nearby leaves and decided not to because I didn't know whether that would disturb them. I also recalled changing how I mow around exposed roots, and how returning to the walnut seems to affect my attention to other people and places. These are not all the same thing: a perceptual interruption, a deliberative restraint, a changed practice, and reported learning across situations need different explanations.

My recent reading of [Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) makes that distinction harder and more interesting. His person–axe–tree circuit and treatment of memory mean that I cannot describe a widened, relationally formed mind as if he hadn't already proposed an ecological account. Husserl's motivated experience, Merleau-Ponty's embodied habits, and Ruyer's temporally organized form remain serious comparisons. A leaf fluttering in the breeze is perceptually given, but it isn't evidence that the tree deliberately released it; nor does my restraint establish what was good for the tree. The constructive problem I now carry into November is whether another living being's *independent ongoing activity* helps realize a conscious intention in a way these accounts don't already explain. I haven't answered that yet.

**October 30 continuation:** In [What Falls and What Carries Forward](../field-notes/2026-10-30%20-%20What%20Falls%20and%20What%20Carries%20Forward.md), the leaf fall continued under a blue sky, and a squirrel and falling acorns interrupted my intended observations. An acorn actually struck my shoulder. Those bodily interruptions aren't the same kind of event as something I described from my morning drive: another car began moving into my lane, and I eased back rather than responding with the horn or lights. I experienced concern for the unknown driver and wondered whether my repeated walnut encounters have changed the ways I respond to other people. This is a concrete reported response beyond the yard, not a controlled before-and-after comparison. My driving experience, concern for the children, prior commitments, and the learning described by Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, and Bateson remain strong alternatives to a distinct theory of intentionality.

Two new sources complicate the case. Simon P. James's 2026 study of experiential diversity distinguishes other beings' possible conscious worlds from species diversity and leaves plant consciousness unsettled. Andrew Trevor Kirkpatrick's 2020 thesis on Whitehead and Merleau-Ponty argues for a process phenomenology and early/late continuity, although I have yet to test its full argument. Both give me reasons not to claim novelty prematurely. I now have a more definite question for November: how might another living being's independently unfolding life help form a later conscious act, and how could I distinguish that role from memory, habit, ethical judgment, or learned motivation? The answer is still open.

**October 31 continuation:** In [Through the Thinning Canopy](../field-notes/2026-10-31%20-%20Through%20the%20Thinning%20Canopy.md), the walnut's canopy has become more yellow, and a falling piece briefly touches my shoulder as I watch more foliage pass through the air. The moon remains visible through openings in the branches. I feel sad about the bare canopy I expect to see soon, although that future has not arrived. These are distinct elements of the encounter: present perception, immediate contact and tracking, remembered prior appearances, and anticipated absence. I cannot infer the tree's interiority or the cause of a particular detachment from what I see.

This is a useful limit on my constructive ambition. A moving artificial piece could also draw and sustain my gaze, and [Husserl's](../concepts/Husserl.md) temporal and motivated syntheses already explain much of the anticipation. [Bateson](../concepts/Gregory%20Bateson.md) can describe a responsive perceptual circuit. [Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) and [Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) supply stronger accounts of living form and individuality, but the philosophical burden is still to show **whether this walnut's ongoing life, rather than a moving stimulus, learned habit, or a later judgment of its good, helps realize a particular conscious act**. I have not yet shown that it does. The accompanying *Ecology of the Cross* proposal about communion without possession must be argued from Christian texts and history, not read out of autumn leaves.

The [public research roadmap](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/blob/main/RESEARCH-ROADMAP.md) now preserves seven questions, including a methodological one about allowing future readers to reconstruct revisions and failed arguments. I want that history to remain visible even if these richer accounts of intentionality defeat my own candidate. A checked task is not a philosophical proof; a carefully preserved correction can still advance the work.

## The two questions

The October 21 Comps 2 outline keeps two explanatory burdens separate. They are related, but answering one will not automatically answer the other.

**First: What does late Merleau-Ponty's ontology of flesh explain beyond Husserl and early Merleau-Ponty?**

[Edmund Husserl](../concepts/Husserl.md) already offers a remarkably rich account of perception. An object appears through partial profiles and changing horizons; the sensing body moves, remembers, anticipates, develops habits, and responds to what draws attention. His account of motivation even allows what I perceive to lead me toward scientific investigation and practical judgment. Early [Maurice Merleau-Ponty](../concepts/Maurice%20Merleau-Ponty.md) makes this challenge stronger through embodied movement, habit, and the bodily *I can*.

In his later work, Merleau-Ponty asks whether sensing and sensible existence must be understood from a different ontological starting point. His language of *flesh*, *chiasm*, incomplete reversibility, and *écart* may explain how perceiver and perceived are differentiated within a sensible world rather than first existing as two complete terms. But that ontological proposal isn't proven by touching a tree, and sharing a sensible world doesn't establish shared conscious experience.

**Second: Even if late flesh contributes something important, what does ecological intentionality contribute beyond these accounts and existing ecological inquiry?**

Distinguishing perception, biological investigation, historical knowledge, and ethical reasoning may be useful without constituting a new kind of intentionality. It may also describe work that phenomenology, natural history, Indigenous relational traditions, ecowomanism, and ecological ethics already do well. I want the term to earn its place, not receive one because it seems to suit a tree.

## What the walnut has made difficult

The practice began by distinguishing what was **perceptually given** from what I remembered, expected, inferred, or brought conceptually to the encounter. That discipline hasn't disappeared as the questions have grown.

In [What a Clearer View Leaves Open](../field-notes/2026-10-19%20-%20What%20a%20Clearer%20View%20Leaves%20Open.md) (October 19), I predicted that moving a little to one side would separate branches that seemed to overlap. The movement fulfilled that prediction. It wasn't a correction of a mistaken belief. A clearer view also didn't disclose the branches' unwitnessed growth or history. Husserl's account of bodily movement and perceptual fulfillment already explains much of this.

In [What Touch Leaves Open](../field-notes/2026-10-20%20-%20What%20Touch%20Leaves%20Open.md) (October 20), I rested my palm, the back of my hand, and my forearm against the walnut's bark. Touch disclosed roughness, pressure, and bodily posture, but not the history of a familiar knot or what, if anything, the walnut experiences. More intimate contact wasn't automatically more adequate knowledge, still less a justified practice of care.

In [A Shared Morning](../field-notes/2026-10-21%20-%20A%20Shared%20Morning.md) (October 21), a familiar protrusion high on the trunk caught my attention differently in the morning light. A squirrel briefly appeared and seemed to look toward me. I imagined its opinion of a person who keeps talking to this tree, then recognized that the imagined commentary was mine. Why the protrusion caught my eye, how it formed, and how learning its history might change what I notice are connected questions requiring different kinds of evidence.

In [Where Growth Meets a Cut](../field-notes/2026-10-22%20-%20Where%20Growth%20Meets%20a%20Cut.md) (October 22), I returned to branches pruned near the power lines. I remember witnessing one cut and asking a worker to be gentle with the walnut. That lets me say something about a particular human event, but not whether the pruning helped or harmed the tree, nor why every other branch ends where it does. Remembering the cause of one cut doesn't establish its consequences or settle what care would require.

In [What Living Changes](../field-notes/2026-10-23%20-%20What%20Living%20Changes.md) (October 23), two photographs of the same branch from positions about ten feet apart opened up different visible details without overturning a belief. I also paused over two smaller branches, one extending upward and the other drooping down, beside the strap holding a swing. All three are attached in a visible sense. Yet the branches belong to the walnut's developing life, while the strap belongs to an organized human practice of play. Their different kinds of participation make a better question than the simple contrast between things with form and things without it. I remember both smaller branches bearing walnuts, but that memory doesn't establish their present health or the effects of the strap.

Across these encounters, a visible connection keeps proving different from a causal explanation, a history, or an argument for care. Something can be perceptually given without being a gift or an invitation. A practice of attention can improve a description without yet establishing what is good for a particular organism.

One methodological conviction has become clearer: **the task isn't merely to expect the unexpected. It's to recognize what actually happened, even when it doesn't serve the argument I brought to the tree.** Changed salience, redirected attention, a corrected belief, and a revised ethical judgment are not the same event.

## Thinkers who complicate the account

Husserl is now my strongest philosophical check against inventing a new category too early. Merleau-Ponty's late ontology may offer a different understanding of the relation between perceiver and perceived, but its contribution must be demonstrated against Husserl and Merleau-Ponty's earlier work.

[Edith Stein](../concepts/Edith%20Stein.md) helps me ask different questions. Her account of empathy insists that another's experience, when given as another's, isn't primordially mine. Her discussion of vegetal phenomena of life allows a qualified kind of apprehension without authorizing confident claims about plant sensation or an awake plant *I*. Her later metaphysics of living form and finite being raises the separate question of a creature's own development and good. I'm not assuming these claims fit neatly with Merleau-Ponty's flesh.

[Raymond Ruyer](../concepts/Ruyer.md) has become a more active challenge. In chapter 9 of *Neofinalism*, his *survol absolu* concerns the immanent unity and active organization of form, not another viewpoint I could reach by walking around the walnut. His further account of primary consciousness is a metaphysical proposal, not something a photograph of branches can establish. Chapter 5 complicates an easy living/nonliving divide: manufactured tools and machines can have organization by form without being living organs. The swing strap is therefore not formless, even though it isn't a growing member of the tree. But having a shape or participating in an arrangement doesn't by itself establish what Ruyer calls a *true form* or an absolute domain of survey. His chapter 14 on physical beings is the next textual test, particularly for questions about rocks, soil, and collections of living and nonliving constituents; I haven't settled those cases by looking at them.

One historical question has also become sharper. Hamrick and Van der Veken report that Merleau-Ponty borrowed the term *survol* from Ruyer in a 1953 course. That's a secondary-source attribution I still need to compare with the course itself. It would be premature to assume that Merleau-Ponty's criticism of *pensée de survol* and Ruyer's *survol absolu* either mean the same thing or simply oppose one another.

The conversation also cannot remain confined to European phenomenology. [Vanessa Watts's](../concepts/Vanessa%20Watts.md) Place-Thought challenges the priority of a human perceiver confronting a surrounding environment, from within particular Indigenous cosmological traditions. [Robin Wall Kimmerer](../concepts/Robin%20Wall%20Kimmerer.md) makes gift, reciprocity, harvest, restoration, and living beings' differentiated needs questions of situated practice. [Melanie Harris's](../concepts/Melanie%20Harris.md) ecowomanism presses the importance of Black women's experience, historical memory, structural harm, environmental justice, and accountable praxis. Their work is not additional evidence for a phenomenological framework already decided elsewhere.

A theological horizon remains open in what I've been calling the *Ecology of the Cross*. Creatureliness, participation, suffering, and redemption matter here, but a broken branch isn't evidence of a Christological claim. [Niels Henrik Gregersen's](../concepts/Niels%20Henrik%20Gregersen.md) questions about evolutionary suffering and deep incarnation are one theological test I still need to develop, not a conclusion the fieldwork can establish. Ecological damage or imposed harm doesn't become good because I can give it a moving theological interpretation.

## What remains open

There are four live possibilities for ecological intentionality. It might name **a distinct intentional structure**, **an education or configuration of inherited capacities**, **a disciplined coordination of inquiries with different warrants**, or **a term whose apparent work is already done elsewhere**. None has won by default. Even the appealing phrase “a discipline of transitions” must answer to existing approaches.

Six questions are carrying the next stage of the work:

- **Ontology:** What does late flesh explain that Husserl and early Merleau-Ponty cannot already explain?
- **Otherness:** How do empirical ignorance, Stein's non-primordiality, and Merleau-Ponty's *écart* differ? When does “intimacy with remainder” conceal those differences?
- **Form and organization:** What makes a living branch, an attached swing strap, or an inorganic composite a unity, and in what sense? Does Ruyer's *true form* explain anything beyond perceived structure and the organizations investigated by biology and physical science? A patch of soil, with its many constituents and lives, must not be treated as one self-surveying organism by assumption.
- **Creaturely goods:** What warrants moving from an observed condition to an account of this organism's flourishing, and then to an ethical obligation? The goods of an individual tree, a species, and a place need not coincide.
- **Place and history:** Whose evidence and experience shape an adequate ecological description? When are biological research, histories, community knowledge, and political analysis necessary?
- **Distinctiveness:** If ecological intentionality adds nothing beyond existing scholarship and practice, am I prepared to relinquish the term?

These are tests of the argument, not rhetorical questions with answers chosen in advance.

## What comes next

The next stage begins now, not in November. I'm attempting a **positive account of ecological intentionality**, using each walnut encounter to articulate one proposition, give its strongest rival explanation, and identify what evidence or argument would defeat it. Husserl, Stein, Merleau-Ponty, and Ruyer remain indispensable, but phenomenology isn't the outer limit of the inquiry. Bateson and Ingold, along with ecological-enactive accounts, are serious predecessors whose primary arguments I need to test rather than claim as my own.

My October 24 reading and source check returned to Ruyer's *Neofinalism*, chapter 14 (pp. 140–153), with a related passage from chapter 13 (p. 137). Ruyer extends active organization into the physical world and even recognizes that events can modify guiding themes; that makes a mere change of human purpose an insufficient claim to novelty. Nor does a fabricated tree become unorganized simply because it lacks the walnut's particular living development. I still need to compare Merleau-Ponty's earlier use of *survol* in its primary context.

In the [October 24 sit](../field-notes/2026-10-24%20-%20A%20Purpose%20the%20Tree%20Does%20Not%20Share.md), a closer look at the swing supplies a small visual detail and an intention to remove it, but not an arboricultural verdict or proof that the tree constitutes the intending. I want to follow this case into a more exact comparison of **the content of an intention, its successful fulfillment, and the conditions that realize the conscious act**. That gives the affirmative philosophical proposal something definite to explain.

Alongside this work, I want to find out what the *Ecology of the Cross* can contribute as a constructive theology of creaturely participation, not just as a biblical illustration of ecological relationships. Its implications for churches and spiritual communities matter, provided they remain accountable to living ecologies, place histories, and whose lives are affected.

I'm not asking the tree to resolve the philosophy. I'm trying to let actual encounters and serious reading make my claims answerable to what I can know, what I still need to investigate, and what I shouldn't infer.
