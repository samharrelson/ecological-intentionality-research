Charles S. Peirce enters the field notes through a selected reading of “Evolutionary Love.” His account helps develop a positive question about care: how might concern encourage another's independent development rather than make the other conform to an already settled purpose?

In the opening of the essay, Peirce describes love as giving creations independence while drawing them into harmony. His example of cherishing an idea connects this movement to the development of thought. Later, he describes environmental interruptions of habit and the way a philosophical idea acquires particular practical cases. These passages challenge [Ecological Intentionality](Ecological%20Intentionality.md) to explain both independence and concrete development without presenting those concerns as unprecedented.

The reciprocal question concerns harmony. The goods of several living beings cannot be assumed to agree because they appear together or because I want to care for them. Whether Peirce's wider account can accommodate genuinely incompatible goods requires argument. The November 5 sit does not establish such a conflict; it identifies what the litter inquiry must remain able to discover.

## What the November 5 sit asks of Peirce

I began with a surprisingly familiar scene: after rain, the walnut's canopy was more open and the ground was far more yellow than I expected. Peirce's account of the development of ideas encourages the passage from a general conception to particular consequences worth investigating. But I need to distinguish two jobs. Explaining why leaves fell requires an inquiry into causal events, while deciding what to do with the litter requires evidence of likely effects and an account of whose good I mean to pursue. The first does not logically determine the second.

Peirce's account of agapic growth also raises a difficulty for my use of **belonging**. His love moves toward another's independence *and* toward harmony. I want to take both terms seriously, without assuming that independently living beings in an ecology have compatible goods or that the moral task is always to resolve differences into one coherent whole. The comparison belongs to the work of theory, not to an empirical claim about the walnut and neighboring vegetation.

Peirce's wider cosmology is not simply a synonym for the phenomenology of intentional action, nor does it do the work of Christian exegesis. Likewise, calling this morning's hypothesis *abduction* is a useful research lead but **the formal logical account requires a separate primary-text comparison**. The selected passages of “Evolutionary Love” currently checked here do not complete that task.
