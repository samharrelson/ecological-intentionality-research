Raymond Ruyer enters the larger project through living form, self-survey, finality, and the question of whether organismic unity requires a metaphysical account stronger than phenomenological constitution alone.

The October 22 sit gives him a direct role: tracing a tree's visible branching structure differs from arguing for its own unity or self-survey. His account rejects a supplementary internal observer and advances a substantive claim about primary consciousness. It cannot be established by the appearance of a branch end. Husserl's and Stein's resources remain strong comparators; what Ruyer adds still has to be argued.

## Works in this project

- *Neofinalism*, trans. Alyosha Edlebi (2016): organismic form, self-survey, finality, and value. Chapter 9 (especially pp. 94, 99–101) advances a claim about active organization and primary consciousness that a tree photograph cannot verify. Chapter 5 (pp. 36–37) argues that the nervous system extends organic finality into an external world of tools and machines, which can be organized by form without being living matter. On p. 44, Ruyer further distinguishes the externally constructed and inspected tool from an organ whose organized living activity is not reducible to its mechanism. That sharpens the swing-strap comparison: a human activity can depend on the walnut's branch without the tree becoming our tool in the same sense as the manufactured swing, an organ of our body, or a co-intender. **Status:** checked against the indexed English primary-text attachment on October 25, including p. 44; page-image collation remains outstanding. This is Ruyer's metaphysical account, not a physiological diagnosis or proof of the new ecological-intentionality hypothesis.

**October 26 primary-text return:** Chapter 14 (particularly pp. 142–143, 147, 150–151) prevents another shortcut. Ruyer treats form as ongoing activity, including in his proposed account of physical individuals, while arguing that a sufficiently integrated whole can organize or relatively subordinate its component individualities. That does **not** make the walnut, its swing, and an observer one self-surveying subject. It sharpens a positive question for ecological intentionality: **Can independently organized beings help sustain intentional activity without being absorbed into a single organizing purpose?** The selected chapter passages were independently rechecked against the indexed English primary-text PDF October 26, but not newly collated against page images; his metaphysics is not direct evidence of vegetal subjectivity or current quantum-physics consensus.

**October 27: temporality and the soil objection.** In chapter 9, p. 100, Ruyer treats organismic form as a unity of processes across **space and time**, not an anatomy held still for inspection. Chapter 11, pp. 112–115, uses Stapledon's imagined future humans to distinguish changing the **meaning** of an earlier event from changing the event itself. Chapter 14, p. 149, describes physical activity in temporal rhythms. These are philosophical claims to understand and challenge, not evidence that the walnut experiences duration like a person. Today's [sit](../field-notes/2026-10-27%20-%20More%20Than%20One%20Time.md) pressed Ruyer with soil and the human microbiome: coordination among living beings does not by itself make them one organism, yet he already has a theory of component subordination (pp. 143, 150–151) that must be taken seriously. **Science correction:** Ruyer does consider changes, fragmentation and even possible cessation of elementary physical domains (pp. 152–153); it would misrepresent him to say that he believes literally immutable atoms are required by his view. Source status: checked against the indexed English primary PDF and printed page labels; no fresh page-image collation or soil microbial study.

**October 28: the branch that no longer hangs.** The [October 28 sit](../field-notes/2026-10-28%20-%20What%20the%20Branch%20Leaves%20Open.md) returns to Ruyer's distinction between a merely visible arrangement and the ongoing unity of a living form. Physical attachment, living membership, and ecological participation aren't identical; neither the photograph nor the removal establishes which tissues were alive or whether the tree benefited. The further question is whether a human intention becomes what it is *through* such independent living activity, beyond a motivated judgment and feedback. Haraway's account of multispecies *making-with* offers a different perspective on relational formation, not a second name for Ruyer's absolute survey.
