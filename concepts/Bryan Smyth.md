Bryan Smyth enters these Field Notes as a scholar of Merleau-Ponty whose interpretation makes the project's claim to distinctiveness harder to establish.

## Work in this project

In “Merleau-Ponty and the ‘Naturalization’ of Phenomenology” (2010), Smyth reads the early postwar Merleau-Ponty as a radical development within transcendental philosophy. Embodied intentionality does not simply replace that inquiry with physiology or naturalistic cognitive science. He also interprets the realization of a common world as a practical and normative task. His permission for empirical work is conditional: its accommodation within phenomenology changes its philosophical meaning. He calls the resulting direction a “phenomenologization of naturalism,” which is stronger than a neutral exchange between methods.

This is secondary scholarship about Merleau-Ponty. It should be tested against the primary texts, including the distinct problem of the unfinished late ontology. For the walnut project, Smyth raises a specific objection: embodiment, openness, and practical orientation do not by themselves establish a new ecological category. The remaining question is how empirical and situated inquiries can correct philosophical descriptions without reducing them to another explanatory register.

## Passages and open objections

The article appears in *Philosophy Today* 54, SPEP Supplement (2010), pp. 153–162. Read p. 157 on methodologically shaped description, pp. 158–159 on universality and praxis, and pp. 159–160 on the conditional place of empirical research. The strongest remaining question is whether empirical resistance can revise a claim about necessary conditions of experience, rather than only a contingent description. A second is how the recognition of situated difference warrants the particular normative task Smyth proposes.

His contrast with Husserl also requires care. Merleau-Ponty's own Preface to *Phenomenology of Perception* explicitly recognizes Husserl's problem of others and situated intersubjectivity. Neither the walnut's independent life nor a change of viewpoint decides that textual dispute.
