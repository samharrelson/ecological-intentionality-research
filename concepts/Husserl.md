Edmund Husserl enters these Field Notes wherever the walnut encounter tests questions of intentionality, perceptual profiles, horizonality, embodiment, temporal synthesis, constitution, and the limits of what is given.

His role here is especially important as a pressure test for [Ecological Intentionality](Ecological%20Intentionality.md): the project should not introduce a new category if Husserl’s account of layered intentional structures already describes the phenomenon adequately. His account of motivation also shows that an appearing thing can stimulate attention and elicit a response from the perceiver without thereby becoming an intentional subject.

**November 6: practical purpose, not only changed scenery.** In [the November 6 sit](../field-notes/2026-11-06%20-%20When%20Another%20Life%20Changes%20the%20End.md), a decision to mow fallen leaves for tidiness was replaced, following ecological reading, by an intention to leave whole litter as overwintering habitat. The action was not carried out during the sit, and the revision preceded the sunshine, squirrels, and witnessed leaf fall. The speaker describes it as a change in what the action is *for*. Husserl's *Ideas II* §§55–56 describes experienced surroundings that motivate attentional and practical action, including goals and valuing. A **changed immediate end under a continuing higher-order commitment** can be compatible with that account rather than a counterexample. More recent phenomenologies of agency further distinguish particular plans from continuing policies and commitments. The burden is not to show Husserl cannot name an ethical beneficiary but to identify an explanatory feature of the constitutive act that motivation cannot already account for.

**Source-history qualification:** James Jardine's 2022 manuscript study suggests that some parts of the published *Ideas II* §56 account may reflect **Edith Stein's editorial elaboration**, alongside substantial motivation analyses traceable to Husserl's manuscripts. That complicates an easy “Husserl lacked, Stein supplied” history. These are scholarly/editorial claims to verify against the critical manuscript edition, not proof of a missing ecological act-type. See [Sources & Reading](../project/sources-and-reading.md).

**November 5 comparison:** The familiar approach past the red maple carried a learned expectation that the altered walnut canopy interrupted. Some leaves were actually seen falling while the prior overnight sequence remained unknown. The resulting interest in leaf litter can be described in Husserlian terms as habit, horizonal expectation, disappointed anticipation, motivation, and practical inquiry. A stronger ecological theory must explain a feature of that *particular act* that this rich description cannot. Even a botanically informed change of management might be a change of means rather than of intended end. A living beneficiary's subsequent benefit is a distinct question from the constitution of the earlier act. ## November 7: a charitable account of this walnut encounter, and where I want to press it

I need to reconstruct Husserl as an actual rival, not as someone who reduces all experience to a solitary spectator receiving neutral visual impressions. Today's [November 7 sit](../field-notes/2026-11-07%20-%20Whose%20Good%20Governs%20the%20Act.md) gives me a relatively definite act: I look closely at ivy climbing the walnut while considering whether I ought to investigate, preserve, manage or eventually remove it.

**First, the object is experienced through more than a visual profile.** A particular tree appears as one enduring thing through changing positions, bodily movements and incomplete perspectives. The bark I see isn't a detached image; its unseen sides are intended within horizons of possible further experience. Walking closer can make an initially unclear growth on the bark more determinate. The vine appears in the same practical situation as the walnut rather than as a bare colored patch. *Ideas II*'s work on material constitution and embodiment therefore does not stop at what is currently visible. The point is not that phenomenological constitution manufactures the physical tree, but that the tree is encountered with a determinable sense and range of anticipations.

**Second, the experience is temporally layered.** The walnut's upper branches appear against remembered fuller canopies, anticipated changes and familiar patterns from repeated visits. That combination of lived present, retention, recollection, expectation and habit does not give direct access to the biological processes between my photographs, but it makes the present intelligible. In *Ideas I* §81 Husserl discusses phenomenological time and time-consciousness, while §§77–82 also address reflection; *Ideas II* §59 and the opening of §60 help distinguish unthematic tendencies from explicitly formed expectations and the practical “I can.” These are different temporal and practical structures, not the same word for “memory.”

**Third, my bodily movement and practical world help structure the act.** I can approach the tree, look up, touch the bark, inspect an ivy leaf, photograph a patch, move away, or return with different tools. Husserl's constitution of the lived body and the personalistic attitude are already accounts of meaningful bodily capacities, not mere physical motion. The walnut is encountered in a yard with uses and values, including soil, family access, play, the tree's continued life, and ecological concerns. Those motives can be present together without forming one coherent purpose.

**Fourth, practical interest is *motivated*, not merely caused.** In *Ideas II* **§55** the experienced surrounding world can stimulate interest, comparison, natural-scientific investigation, practical formation and decisions about values or goals. **§56(a)** concerns the confirmation and possible cancellation of judgments through experience; **§56(f)** distinguishes understanding a motivated personal act from explaining its natural causes. That latter distinction does not mean biological inquiry cannot change a person's beliefs or reasons. It says that the intelligibility of my action *as motivated* is not the same type of explanation as the physiology of the ivy's growth.

I can therefore give the following account: I habitually return to the walnut, recognize a vine climbing the familiar bark, learn to identify it and remember my earlier experience with other vines. The plant's appearance and the research motivate practical concern. I entertain possible future actions with several beneficiaries and costs. Additional biological information changes my evaluations, and I adopt a different projected end or choose to wait. Husserl's layered account can describe the development of that conscious sequence. De Monticelli's analysis of commitments and plans, and Barber's presentation of Schütz's *in-order-to* motives, make an even more explicit alternative account of practical purpose.

**My positive objection remains, but it has to be specific.** The actual walnut has a continuing biological life that an artificial model does not. It could be harmed, helped or altered by my actions in ways the model itself cannot. My history of encounter with its changing life matters to the very ends I now consider. I still suspect this is more than simply attaching a new ethical value to an object. But a photograph of the **actual** tree could mediate concern for it, while a LEGO model might motivate care for real trees elsewhere. The medium's lack of organic life isn't the same as a nonliving intended beneficiary. And the independence of the tree as a condition of *whether care succeeds* is not automatically an additional constituent of *my act of intending*. That is the remaining constructive burden.

The **current comparison does not yet defeat this charitable Husserlian account**. It identifies where I think a stronger ecological theory must be formulated and tested: whether the independent activity of an encountered life enters the ongoing realization of a particular intention in a way better understanding, motive, valuation, embodiment and subsequent normative correction cannot exhaust.

**Primary and source status:** Husserl, *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology and to a Phenomenological Philosophy*, First Book, §§77–84 (especially §81 for the time problem, §84 intentionality); Second Book: *Studies in the Phenomenology of Constitution*, trans. Richard Rojcewicz and André Schuwer (Kluwer, 1989), especially **§§50, 55–56, 59–60**, selected passages previously examined in the primary Zotero copy. This is a **working charitable reconstruction**, not a claim that every section has been reread contiguously today or its printed pages re-collated. James Jardine, *Empathy, Embodiment, and the Person* (2022), **p. 180 n. 25**, provisionally attributes significant editorial elaboration within transmitted §56 to Edith Stein while acknowledging substantive Husserl manuscripts on motivation. That editorial-history thesis remains to be checked against the relevant critical edition. See [Sources & Reading](../project/sources-and-reading.md).

**Research roadmap criterion:** This account was drafted for the first remaining test in [Issue #1](https://github.com/samharrelson/ecological-intentionality-research/issues/1). Its publication and source-adequacy must be verified before marking that criterion complete. The second criterion, an actual act-level discriminating comparison or explicit demonstrated failure, also needs its own defensible publication. A longer field note isn't by itself sufficient for either.

- *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology and to a Phenomenological Philosophy, Second Book*: especially sensuous synthesis, embodiment, constitution, motivation, the surrounding world, cultural and practical objects, the distinction between intentional and real-causal relation, and the redetermination or cancellation of an apprehension through further experience. Sept. 27 adds a scale question: sensuous synthesis can explain **one perceived thing**, while Husserl’s broader constitutive program also contemplates animate / living strata. Sept. 28 adds another correction: the personalistic attitude can give an object *as* a practical-cultural object rather than as neutral sensory material. October 6 adds a temporal control from *Ideas I* §§77–82: retention is not recollection, immediate protention is not explicit anticipation, and every present experience carries horizons of before and after. October 7 returns to *Ideas II* around pp. 35–40 and §18, where unseen but co-meant sides can become more fully given through a course of perception and bodily movement belongs constitutively to that disclosure. October 8 turns the temporal distinction into an evidential one: an unwitnessed event does not become recollection merely because its aftermath is intelligible within a familiar horizon. The remaining limits are that **cultural givenness is not historical explanation, temporal identity is not yet living development, and perceptual fulfillment is not biological explanation**.

## November 8: a historical person is already a Husserlian subject

The [November 8 sit](https://ecologicalintentionality.org/sits/2026-11-08) follows how enduring concerns become more specific across separate encounters and later actions. A further targeted Zotero retrieval has made Husserl's response stronger. James Jardine, *Empathy, Embodiment, and the Person* (2022), chapter 6, especially §6.3 (working pp. 181–185), reconstructs the way position-takings, judgments, affects and resolutions sediment into a person's **habitual character** and later self-understanding. Jardine points to Husserliana IV/V 339–356 and 579–580. These are **selected indexed readings** not a new complete source-to-source verification. They make it harder to claim that phenomenology explains only a static consciousness confronting the walnut.

The remaining test isn't whether reading or living encounter can change my habits. It's whether something about the organism's independent life, or the normativity of care for its actual good, requires an account beyond this robust genetic description. A photograph can mediate attention to a real walnut; a meaningful LEGO artifact can also shape the history of an agent's projects. Different referents do not by themselves demonstrate different act-structures.
