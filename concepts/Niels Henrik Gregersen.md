Niels Henrik Gregersen enters the larger inquiry through evolutionary suffering, the cross, and *deep incarnation*. His work raises a theological question that my black walnut encounters cannot answer on their own: how might Christian theology speak about God's relation to vulnerable biological life without interpreting creaturely suffering as deserved punishment or making suffering itself a necessary good?

I am treating Gregersen as a **theological interlocutor whose argument I still need to finish reading**, not as a phenomenological explanation of what I experience beside the walnut. The existence of decay, broken branches, and other ecological losses does not establish that any individual creature experiences pain, or that God suffers with it.

## November 20: participation and its scope

In [The Lives That Form Us](../field-notes/2026-11-20%20-%20The%20Lives%20That%20Form%20Us.md), I draw on Gregersen to develop how participation can form capacities for action. In §I of his 2001 essay, “How Illusory are Human Capacities?”, his reading of Luther connects received grace and faith with becoming able to act in love and justice, responsive to others' needs. Later in the essay, he makes divine transformation central to redemption beyond subjective consolation and describes creaturely preservation within unity with God. I attribute the first account to Gregersen's interpretation of Luther; I haven't independently checked those Luther passages.

The 2015 introduction's concluding discussion after C3.3 also gives me a positive account of difference: participation in divine life varies with the recipient, with sparrow, bonobo and human being as his examples. I use this to develop creaturely participation without making every life interchangeable. My proposal that participation can form a community's capacity to learn, deliberate and share responsibility is a further ecclesial argument. I still need to compare his 2001 language of absorption and preserved essence with the nonpossessive participation I want to defend.

I also keep incarnation's reach distinct from final salvation. C2 includes grass, trees, soil and moisture within the shared conditions of incarnation; C3 denies a strict logical implication from deep incarnation to universalism. My hope for transformed communion needs its own defense. Gregersen's claims about suffering's place in evolution remain a difficulty to engage, while neither source makes the walnut's appearance evidence of pain or redemption. These are selected primary-text checks; my complete reading and page-image collation remain ongoing.

## November 19: carrying care through a disruption

In [What Holds a Purpose Together](../field-notes/2026-11-19%20-%20What%20Holds%20a%20Purpose%20Together.md), the church's move to pickup dinner gives me a concrete question about how a community carries care through changed arrangements. Food provision continued while the conversations I valued became unavailable. I read that experience beside Paul's examination of a communal meal in 1 Corinthians 11:17–34. My comparison concerns the relation between a practice's professed purpose and what it accomplishes. Gregersen's introduction reports Richard Bauckham's distinction between Christ's presence in, with and for creatures and identifying God as every harmful event. That distinction lets me ask how participation in Christ could shape a response without treating the disruption as a divine lesson. Extending the account to other creatures requires attention to their particular lives and goods. Theology can help form the purpose of care; the adequacy of that care still needs investigation.

## Works and questions in this project

- **[“The Cross of Christ in an Evolutionary World.”](https://doi.org/10.1111/0012-2033.00075)** *Dialog: A Journal of Theology* 40, no. 3 (2001): 192–207. I use §I, “How Illusory are Human Capacities?”, for Gregersen's reading of received grace and active agency, and §§II–V for the distinctions among kinds of suffering, his limited evolutionary theodicy, active transformation and creaturely preservation. I still need to assess the full argument.
- Niels Henrik Gregersen, **[“Introduction,”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt13wwwk5.4)** in *Incarnation: On the Scope and Depth of Christology*, ed. Niels Henrik Gregersen (Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2015), 1–21. I use the opening's distinction, attributed to Richard Bauckham, between Christ present *in*, *with* and *for* creation and identifying God *as* every event. C2 supplies incarnation's extended scope; C3.1–C3.3 distinguishes the alternatives for final salvation. The concluding recipient-mode paragraph supports differentiated participation.

This makes several comparisons unavoidable. Stein's creaturely form and received being do not settle the theological origin or meaning of natural suffering. Merleau-Ponty's flesh is not the doctrine of incarnation. And the possibility that God is with suffering creatures would not imply that their suffering is good, necessary, or retrospectively justified by redemption.

Gregersen may also have already done some of the theological work that my proposed *Ecology of the Cross* hopes to do. That's a substantive prior-work objection, not just a source of supportive quotations. I want to compare his account with Maximus the Confessor, Bonaventure, Jürgen Moltmann, and the inherited theology of the cross without declaring those accounts compatible in advance.

**Question carried forward:** How can an account of participation or divine presence remain answerable to particular creaturely goods and losses without turning suffering into the price of a harmonized ecological or theological whole?

The particular lives involved still require ecological inquiry; their benefit cannot be supplied by the theological interpretation.

- [November 12, 2026: Who Asked for the Ring](../field-notes/2026-11-12%20-%20Who%20Asked%20for%20the%20Ring.md): Selected primary passages from the 2015 introduction give the proposed theology of return an incarnational starting point and a limit on identifying God with harmful events. The complete chapter and its typology remain to be read. Elizabeth Johnson's essay in the same volume, "Jesus and the Cosmos: Soundings in Deep Christology," observes that John 1:14 says σάρξ (flesh) where it could have said ἄνθρωπος (human being), and then notes that the flesh the Word became was specifically human. I'm keeping both halves of that.
