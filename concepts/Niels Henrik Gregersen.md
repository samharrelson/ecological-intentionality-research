Niels Henrik Gregersen enters the larger inquiry through evolutionary suffering, the cross, and *deep incarnation*. His work raises a theological question that my black walnut encounters cannot answer on their own: how might Christian theology speak about God's relation to vulnerable biological life without interpreting creaturely suffering as deserved punishment or making suffering itself a necessary good?

I am treating Gregersen as a **theological interlocutor whose argument I still need to finish reading**, not as a phenomenological explanation of what I experience beside the walnut. The existence of decay, broken branches, and other ecological losses does not establish that any individual creature experiences pain, or that God suffers with it.

## Works and questions in this project

- **“The Cross of Christ in an Evolutionary World.”** *Dialog: A Journal of Theology* 40, no. 3 (2001): 192–207. https://doi.org/10.1111/0012-2033.00075. The introduction challenges explanations of evolutionary suffering that simply appeal to human sin, while also questioning an account of creation whose goodness depends on being “crossed.” My close reading so far establishes the article's opening program; its later theological arguments remain to be worked through before I treat them as conclusions.
- **“Introduction.”** In *Incarnation: On the Scope and Depth of Christology*, edited by Niels Henrik Gregersen (Fortress Press, 2015), 1–21. The opening develops the scope of *deep incarnation* while distinguishing Christ present *in*, *with*, and *for* creation from the claim that God is incarnate *as* every event, including violence and disaster. The remaining chapters and full argument require further reading.

This makes several comparisons unavoidable. Stein's creaturely form and received being do not settle the theological origin or meaning of natural suffering. Merleau-Ponty's flesh is not the doctrine of incarnation. And the possibility that God is with suffering creatures would not imply that their suffering is good, necessary, or retrospectively justified by redemption.

Gregersen may also have already done some of the theological work that my proposed *Ecology of the Cross* hopes to do. That's a substantive prior-work objection, not just a source of supportive quotations. I want to compare his account with Maximus the Confessor, Bonaventure, Jürgen Moltmann, and the inherited theology of the cross without declaring those accounts compatible in advance.

**Question carried forward:** How can an account of participation or divine presence remain answerable to particular creaturely goods and losses without turning suffering into the price of a harmonized ecological or theological whole?

Elizabeth Johnson's essay in the same volume, "Jesus and the Cosmos: Soundings in Deep Christology," observes that John 1:14 says σάρξ (flesh) where it could have said ἄνθρωπος (human being), and then notes that the flesh the Word became was specifically human.
