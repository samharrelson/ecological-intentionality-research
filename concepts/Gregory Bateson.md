I've been reading Gregory Bateson as a serious challenge to my proposed account of [ecological intentionality](Ecological%20Intentionality.md). In *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, particularly “Form, Substance, and Difference,” he describes a person–axe–tree circuit through which differences in the cut, perception, bodily movement, and the next stroke all help explain what happens. The appropriate boundary of the mental system depends on the activity we're trying to understand.

That means I can't claim that mind extends beyond an isolated individual as though Bateson hadn't already argued for it. His account is stronger than an environment merely influencing a finished subject. He also includes relevant memory, which makes my claim that encounters with the walnut affect how I notice things elsewhere a question for Bateson, not an immediate objection to him.

What I still want to investigate is whether the particular *conscious intention* and the walnut's independently developing life and possible good demand an account beyond informational feedback, memory, and learning. Bateson's circuit doesn't require the tree, axe, and human to share a single phenomenal perspective. Nor have I established that his account neglects the tree's own life. The difference between system organization and the lives of its participants is precisely what I need to test.

## November 15: learning, memory and the wider circuit

Bateson helps me explain how a history of encounters can shape what I notice and how I act. He also gives me reasons to expect that a formed disposition can resist correction. The distinctions below strengthen the positive account I am trying to write without assigning every reported change to a learning level in advance.

**Source status:** An assisted primary-text pass on November 15 checked the passages and printed page references below against the Jason Aronson 1987 reprint. My own slow rereading remains on the working reading list.

**The learning levels distinguish kinds of change.** In “The Logical Categories of Learning and Communication,” zero learning names a specific response that is not corrected. Learning I changes a response within a set of alternatives. Learning II changes how Learning I proceeds, including its alternatives or its recognition of contexts. Learning III changes the process of Learning II (p. 298). These distinctions let me ask what changed in a purpose formed through a relationship. That formation alone does not identify a learning level.

Bateson explains how learned ways of punctuating experience can become self-validating because my own conduct helps produce the context I expect (pp. 305–306). That account bears on the walnut becoming the center through which I organize the yard. It does not make a factual attribution such as “this branch came from the walnut” immune to being wrong. His claim concerns the framing of experience, not a suspension of truth and error in every judgment made within it.

**Changing a premise is not enough to demonstrate Learning III.** Bateson explicitly allows replacement of premises acquired through Learning II without a change at Level III. His four examples of therapeutic intervention occur within that distinction. He describes Learning III as difficult and rare, and associates it with greater flexibility in how such premises are formed and revised (pp. 307–310). Hearing the children's reasons, turning toward the oak and recognizing the ring's omission are useful cases to examine. They do not by themselves establish Learning III or reproduce a completed therapeutic sequence.

**Memory and distributed circuits belong together.** In “Form, Substance, and Difference,” the explanatory boundary follows relevant pathways through tree, person and axe, rather than stopping at the skin. Bateson also includes relevant memory and data banks. Alongside stored information, a simple circuit has dynamic memory: in his steam-engine governor example, an earlier action affects later behavior after information has traveled around the circuit (pp. 465–466). The present response therefore depends partly on the system's preceding activity. The account is already temporal; an instantaneous stimulus and isolated response would leave part of it unexplained. This passage supplies a model of dynamic memory, not a complete account of every form of recollection or long-term habit.

“The Cybernetics of 'Self'” denies unilateral control by one part and develops the tree and blind-person-with-stick examples (pp. 321–324). “Pathologies of Epistemology” identifies the unit of survival and mind as organism together with environment (pp. 488–490). I should reconstruct that proposal as Bateson's account, while keeping its biological and philosophical adequacy open to assessment. Applied to the walnut sits, these resources require me to trace the relevant perceptual, remembered, practical and environmental connections rather than infer an ahistorical observer from the fact that I begin my description with a present encounter.

**An unplanned correction still falls within Bateson’s scope.** In “Effects of Conscious Purpose on Human Adaptation,” Bateson already considers discrepant organismic purposes, selective consciousness and possible correction through love, art, contact with animals and the natural world, and religion. His account of contact with the natural world is expressly qualified (pp. 450–454). My regular practice, questions, reading and conversations also help arrange what can reach me. The walnut's leaf fall exceeds what I arranged, but that fact does not place the event outside Bateson's resources.

**What I can construct with these resources.** I can trace how an encountered difference enters an existing concern, how earlier experience makes it significant, and whether it changes a factual judgment, a means, an intended beneficiary, a purpose or a way of deciding together. These are different explanatory tasks. A change can be ecological in its formation while remaining selective, resistant or harmful; the independent adequacy of care still requires evidence.

For my developing account, the next comparison is therefore about what particular living beings contribute to the direction and possible fulfillment of an intention, and how their conditions enter communal deliberation. Bateson already gives differentiated organisms and their purposes a place. I need to show whether phenomenological description and the explicit treatment of creaturely goods add an explanation, specify his account, or make an ethical extension. A named other with something at stake is a starting point for that comparison, not a demonstrated omission in Bateson.

## November 8: Learning II and the history of changed questions

The [November 8 field note](https://ecologicalintentionality.org/sits/2026-11-08) asks how repeated encounter, reading and practical decisions can change **what I learn to consider relevant**. Bateson's person–axe–tree circuit is only part of his answer. His earlier “Social Planning and the Concept of Deutero-Learning” (in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, printed essay starts p. 166) and later “The Logical Categories of Learning and Communication” (starts p. 284) develop learning at different levels, including changes in how contexts and expectations are recognized. The selected indexed text warns that Bateson's numbering changes across these essays. **These are new reading priorities, not completed close comparisons.**

If I claim that my relation to the walnut has changed which practical questions arise for me, a Batesonian account of learning-to-learn is a powerful precedent. What remains to examine is whether differentiated organismic goods and the actual human act of intending require an explanation not already offered by situated learning and wider informational circuits. A circuit extending beyond the body is not thereby one shared conscious subject.
