description: Field notes related to ecological intentionality.
---
Ecological intentionality is the working name for a disciplined form of creaturely attention: a way of attending to beings as relationally given, irreducibly other, and never exhausted by their appearance within human experience.

These field notes function as one of the places where that proposal is tested rather than merely illustrated. The current pressure is whether ecological intentionality names anything phenomenologically new at all, or whether it is better understood as a disciplined coordination of already available intentional structures in which perception, interpretation, and care remain open to correction. The Sept. 25–27 sits add a stronger constraint: ecological relation must preserve **integrity without isolation; relation without dissolution; good without guaranteed harmony**. Relations may condition which possibilities a living being can actualize without thereby constituting its norm, and strong collective coordination must not silently be redescribed as one organism. **Do not let ecology borrow the unity of the organism without argument.** Sept. 28 widens the test: if the term survives, it may need to coordinate phenomenological clarification with biological, historical, legal, institutional, political, metaphysical, and ethical inquiry. Husserl prevents phenomenology from being reduced to bare sensory description; the open problem is whether ecological intentionality names a disciplined coordination among methods with different evidential tasks rather than a new intentional structure. Sept. 29 adds a living-boundary discipline: **conditioning is not incorporation, exchange is not flourishing, and permeability is not indistinction.** Oct. 2 adds Harris’s praxis test while Merleau-Ponty’s later account blocks an easy contrast between embodiment and history. His social world and conditioned freedom already make embodied existence historical; Harris presses further by asking whose experience and memory guide ecological knowing, how unequal access and harm are analyzed, and how inquiry moves toward environmental justice and praxis. Oct. 3 makes the terminological pressure explicit: the emerging method coordinates perception, memory, biology, historical evidence, community knowledge, ethical judgment, and praxis. That may be a disciplined ecological orientation rather than a new intentional structure; the name should remain revisable. Oct. 4 shifts the goal from perceiving more connection to differentiating kinds of relation, participants, scales, vulnerabilities, and goods. A post-sit return to Merleau-Ponty makes the pressure sharper: flesh already preserves noncoincidence and rejects a larger-organism model, so the remaining problem is not merely whether belonging preserves difference but whether it can distinguish creaturely goods. **Belonging is not beneficence, and a shared world is not yet one shared good.** Oct. 5 adds another evidential ladder: a shared condition does not establish identical local exposure; differently situated exposure does not establish patterned vulnerability; patterned vulnerability still requires historical and structural explanation before an injustice claim is warranted; and warranted diagnosis is not yet accountable response. Oct. 6 turns that pattern into a more explicit hypothesis: ecological intentionality may be less a new structure than a **discipline of transitions** among perception, memory, anticipation, biological development, metaphysical living form, historical explanation, and stronger ontological or normative claims. The term earns its keep only if it coordinates these transitions without mistaking one level for the next.
