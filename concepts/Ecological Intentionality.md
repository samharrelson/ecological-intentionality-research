description: Field notes related to ecological intentionality.

**I'm now developing ecological intentionality as a positive philosophical proposal about how individual and shared consciousness may come to be through relations with a world we cannot possess.** Other beings need not share our intentions to participate in shaping our possibilities of intending, yet their own activities, histories, and goods must not be assimilated to ours. This remains a candidate account of ecological *constitution*, not an established universal field of consciousness, an attribution of human intention to trees, or a synonym for ethical attention.

Earlier phases of this inquiry emphasized a disciplined form of creaturely attention: attending to beings as relationally given, irreducibly other, and never exhausted by their appearance within human experience. That phenomenological and methodological achievement remains, but it no longer determines the ceiling of the constructive theory. The major unresolved question is whether the ecological relations are *constituents* of conscious activity, not merely its causes, objects, incentives, conditions of successful care, or occasions for learning. [The October 24 sit](../field-notes/2026-10-24%20-%20A%20Purpose%20the%20Tree%20Does%20Not%20Share.md) gives that distinction one concrete intention to test. Husserl, Ruyer, Bateson, Ingold, Watts, Harris, and others are interlocutors and independent challenges, not a preselected synthesis.

**October 25 constructive test:** [The Branch and the Purpose](../field-notes/2026-10-25%20-%20The%20Branch%20and%20the%20Purpose.md) returns to an intention not yet fulfilled. The swing's use brings human memories and purposes into the encounter; the tree has its own developmental conditions, which I have not diagnosed. Ruyer's account of externally organized tools and living organs sharpens what must be explained: **reliance on another living form is not yet proof that the other's life constitutes my intending**. The positive theory has to specify that constitutive relation or revise its ambition. The methodological alternatives traced below remain live objections from earlier stages, not the predetermined endpoint of the inquiry.

**October 29 continuation:** In [What the Leaves Change](../field-notes/2026-10-29%20-%20What%20the%20Leaves%20Change.md), I distinguished a surprise that captured my gaze, a deliberate decision not to touch nearby leaves, my change in mowing practice, and my report that I now approach people and places differently. They may arise through different processes. Bateson's ecological circuit and memory, Husserl's motivated attention, and Merleau-Ponty's learned bodily orientation already explain considerable reorganization without adding a new category. The stronger question is whether this walnut's *independent continuing life* helps realize a particular intention in a way those explanations cannot accommodate. A falling leaf and a cautious gesture don't decide that question. [Bateson](Gregory%20Bateson.md) now has his own source page for this comparison.

These field notes function as one of the places where the developing proposal is tested rather than merely illustrated. In the earlier September–October methodological phase, an important question was whether ecological intentionality named anything phenomenologically new or instead a disciplined coordination of already available intentional structures. That possibility remains a serious **rival explanation**, but the present positive philosophical proposal asks more broadly whether independently organized lives can participate in the realization of intending without sharing its purposes. It is not restricted to what phenomenology permits us to describe. The Sept. 25–27 sits add a stronger constraint: ecological relation must preserve **integrity without isolation; relation without dissolution; good without guaranteed harmony**. Relations may condition which possibilities a living being can actualize without thereby constituting its norm, and strong collective coordination must not silently be redescribed as one organism. **Do not let ecology borrow the unity of the organism without argument.** Sept. 28 widens the test: if the term survives, it may need to coordinate phenomenological clarification with biological, historical, legal, institutional, political, metaphysical, and ethical inquiry. Husserl prevents phenomenology from being reduced to bare sensory description; the open problem is whether ecological intentionality names a disciplined coordination among methods with different evidential tasks rather than a new intentional structure. Sept. 29 adds a living-boundary discipline: **conditioning is not incorporation, exchange is not flourishing, and permeability is not indistinction.** Oct. 2 adds Harris’s praxis test while Merleau-Ponty’s discussion within *Phenomenology of Perception* blocks an easy contrast between embodiment and history. His social world and conditioned freedom already make embodied existence historical; Harris presses further by asking whose experience and memory guide ecological knowing, how unequal access and harm are analyzed, and how inquiry moves toward environmental justice and praxis. Oct. 3 makes the terminological pressure explicit: the emerging method coordinates perception, memory, biology, historical evidence, community knowledge, ethical judgment, and praxis. That may be a disciplined ecological orientation rather than a new intentional structure; the name should remain revisable. Oct. 4 shifts the goal from perceiving more connection to differentiating kinds of relation, participants, scales, vulnerabilities, and goods. A post-sit return to Merleau-Ponty makes the pressure sharper: flesh already preserves noncoincidence and rejects a larger-organism model, so the remaining problem is not merely whether belonging preserves difference but whether it can distinguish creaturely goods. **Belonging is not beneficence, and a shared world is not yet one shared good.** Oct. 5 adds another evidential ladder: a shared condition does not establish identical local exposure; differently situated exposure does not establish patterned vulnerability; patterned vulnerability still requires historical and structural explanation before an injustice claim is warranted; and warranted diagnosis is not yet accountable response. Oct. 6 turns that pattern into a more explicit hypothesis: ecological intentionality may be less a new structure than a **discipline of transitions** among perception, memory, anticipation, biological development, metaphysical living form, historical explanation, and stronger ontological or normative claims. The term earns its keep only if it coordinates these transitions without mistaking one level for the next. A related possibility now emerges from [Remainder](Remainder.md): **transition can move something from one kind of remainder to another**. What is hidden can become visible, what is visible can become remembered, and what becomes perceptually available can still leave biological, historical, experiential, or normative questions unresolved. Oct. 7 gives the hypothesis a concrete field test: changing position fulfilled one hidden branch profile while leaving physiological process and historical explanation untouched. Ecological intentionality may therefore train not the overcoming of remainder but the ability to recognize **how remainder changes status as an encounter develops**, what warrant was gained, and which inquiry must follow. The October 7 source pass raises the burden again: Husserl already explains bodily profile-fulfillment, and a May 1960 Merleau-Ponty working note already distinguishes several kinds of invisibility. If the term earns its keep, it will have to do more than rename either achievement. October 8 adds an identity constraint: **similarity is not identity, and serial observation is not an established individual history.** It also separates bodily capacity from ethical warrant. A disciplined ecological inquiry has to know not only when a relation changes, but what evidence establishes the change and what further claim still does not follow.

October 13 adds a redundancy test for the methodological proposal itself. [Bryan Smyth](Bryan%20Smyth.md) already reads early Merleau-Ponty as a practical and unfinished transcendental project. Distinguishing kinds of warrant is therefore insufficient: the inquiry must show how one claim can be corrected by another kind of evidence, and whether that correction changes a description, a practice, or the framework's terms.
