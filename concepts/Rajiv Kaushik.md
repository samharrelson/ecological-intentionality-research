Rajiv Kaushik enters these Field Notes through a demanding interpretation of Maurice Merleau-Ponty's *écart*. Rather than treating divergence as a distance between beings already complete in themselves, he asks whether differentiation belongs to the constitution of identities and of the sensible field itself.

That interpretation matters to my first explanatory burden: **what does late Merleau-Ponty's ontology actually add beyond Husserl and early Merleau-Ponty?** Kaushik supplies a possible answer, not a conclusion I can borrow without testing.

## Work in this project

- Kaushik, Rajiv. *Merleau-Ponty between Philosophy and Symbolism: The Matrixed Ontology*. Albany: State University of New York Press, 2019. [Publisher information](https://sunypress.edu/isbn/9781438476759).

In the introduction (especially pp. xxi–xxiv) and chapter 1, “Matrix Events: Methods and Antecedents” (pp. 1–29), Kaushik develops an interpretation of the symbolic matrix and Merleau-Ponty's diacritical *écart*. On his account, divergence is not merely an unfilled gap between independently constituted things. The sensible and insensible, identities and differences, and the division between language and world have to be thought through the differentiations that allow them to appear as such.

This is more demanding than the claim that the world is interconnected. It may give late flesh an ontological task not exhausted by descriptions of shifting perceptual profiles or bodily movement.

**The objection matters just as much.** Merleau-Ponty's own working notes also describe an “advent of difference” on a ground of resemblance. That resists an easy claim that difference is absolutely prior to every kind of unity. Nor does a diacritical sensible field, by itself, establish the proper good of a walnut, the squirrel's experience, or an obligation of care. These remain different questions.

I need to compare Kaushik's interpretation with *The Visible and the Invisible* itself and with Husserl's more developed accounts of embodiment, constitution, passive synthesis, and temporality. My present source work is concentrated on the introduction and selected passages of chapter 1; it is not a completed adjudication of the full book.

**Question carried forward:** Does *écart* explain the arising of differences rather than only their experienced noncoincidence? If so, what philosophical consequence survives Husserl's strongest account, and how does it avoid turning every kind of difference into the same ontological claim?
